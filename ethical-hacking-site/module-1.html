The CIA Triad

Core goals

C Confidentiality

Only authorized parties can see the data. Enforced by encryption, access control, least-privilege. Broken by disclosure, sniffing, shared creds.

I Integrity

Data is accurate and unaltered by unauthorized parties. Enforced by hashing, checksums, signatures. Broken by tampering, MITM modification.

A Availability

Authorized users can access data/systems when needed. Enforced by redundancy, patching, DDoS protection. Broken by DoS, ransomware, hardware failure.

Threat Modeling & Risk

Thinking like a defender

Security work is about managing risk, not eliminating it. Risk = Likelihood × Impact: how probable an attack is, times how bad it would be. You spend effort where the product is highest.

Spoofing

Impersonating a user/system (fake identity).

Tampering

Altering data/code (breaks Integrity).

Repudiation

Denying an action was taken.

Info disclosure

Leaking data (breaks Confidentiality).

Denial of service

Breaking Availability.

Elevation

Gaining higher privileges than allowed.

Defense in depth in practice: pair controls so one failure doesn't sink the ship — firewall + IDS + patching + training.

Phases

1Reconnaissance

Passive + active info gathering (footprinting, scanning) — map the target. See M2.

2Scanning & Enumeration

Find live hosts, open ports, services, users. See M2.

3Gaining access

Exploit a vulnerability to get in (SQLi, buffer overflow, phishing). See M3/M4.

5Covering tracks

Clear logs, hide files, wipe evidence. See M5.

+Skills of a hacker

Curiosity, scripting, networking, OS internals, patience, methodical note-taking.

What happens at each phase (worked view)

  1. Reconnaissance — attacker builds an attack-surface map: domains, IPs, staff, tech stack (no packets touch the target in passive recon).
  2. Scanning & Enumeration — Nmap finds 10.0.0.5:443 open; banner grab shows outdated web server; SMTP VRFY lists valid usernames.
  3. Gaining access — attacker exploits the outdated server (or sends a phishing link) and lands a shell as a low-priv user.
  4. Maintaining access — drops a scheduled-task backdoor and escalates to admin so re-entry survives a reboot.
  5. Covering tracks — clears the Security event log and timestomps dropped files before leaving.
Mnemonic for the phases: Recon → Scan → Gain → Maintain → Cover (RSGMC). Every later module is one of these phases in detail.
03

Reconnaissance in Practice

Phase 1, detailed

Before any exploit, an attacker maps the target. Two styles:

Passive (low risk of detection)

  • OSINT — WHOIS, DNS records, certificate transparency logs
  • Social media, job postings (tech stack leaks), LinkedIn
  • Search-engine dorking, archived sites (Wayback)
  • Shodan / passive DNS for exposed assets

Active (touches the target)

  • Port scanning, OS & service fingerprinting
  • Banner grabbing, traceroute
  • More detectable — may trip IDS/IPS
The goal is an attack surface map: live hosts, open ports, versions, users, and the easiest entry (often a person, not a port).

01

What Is a Penetration Test?

Authorized attack

A penetration test (pen test) is an authorized, simulated attack on a system to evaluate its security. Testers use the same tools/techniques as attackers to find and demonstrate the business impact of weaknesses.

Types by knowledge of the target

Black-box

No internal knowledge — simulates an external attacker. Most realistic, slowest.

White-box

Full knowledge (source, arch, creds) — deepest coverage, fastest, highest assurance.

Grey-box

Partial knowledge — a balance of realism and efficiency.

Pen test vs vulnerability assessment: a vuln assessment finds and lists weaknesses (often automated); a pen test goes further and exploits them to prove real impact.

Scoping & Rules of Engagement

Before you test

A real engagement is a written contract, not a spontaneous hack. Key clauses:

  • Scope — exact IPs, URLs, apps in (and out of) bounds.
  • Timing — windows when testing is allowed.
  • Test type — black/grey/white box agreed upfront.
  • Out-of-bounds — no DoS, no real PII exfil, no social engineering unless permitted.
  • Reporting — confidential delivery to a named contact.
Pen test vs vulnerability assessment: a vuln scan is automated and lists "you have CVE-X"; a pen test proves "CVE-X lets me reach domain admin," with business impact.

E1

Confidentiality in Depth

Deep dive · mechanism

Confidentiality is the promise that information is disclosed only to those who are entitled to see it. It is the property most people think of first when they hear the word security, but in practice it is enforced by a chain of controls rather than one switch. The chain begins with classification: decide what is public, internal, confidential, and restricted, because you cannot protect what you have not labelled. It continues with access control, which decides who may read a given object, and ends with cryptography, which keeps data unreadable even if it is stolen.

Encryption protects data in two states. Data at rest is stored on a disk, database, or backup tape; full-disk encryption and file- or column-level encryption are typical controls. Data in transit crosses a network; TLS, IPsec, and SSH create an encrypted tunnel so a passive observer sees only ciphertext. Symmetric encryption uses one shared key (AES is the modern standard) and is fast, so it protects bulk data. Asymmetric encryption uses a public/private key pair (RSA, ECC) and is slower, so it is used to exchange symmetric keys and to prove identity rather than to encrypt large files.

Access-control models answer the question "who decides?" In a discretionary model (DAC) the owner of a resource grants permissions at their own discretion, which is flexible but weak. In a mandatory model (MAC) a central policy labels both subjects and objects and the system enforces the rule regardless of the owner, which is what military and government systems use. Role-based access control (RBAC) assigns permissions to roles rather than individuals, so a person inherits rights by joining a team and loses them by leaving it. Attribute-based access control (ABAC) makes decisions from attributes such as department, time of day, and device posture. In every model the guiding principle is least privilege: give each subject only the access it needs, for only as long as it needs it.

AES-256 disk encryptionAt restReadable files if the laptop is stolenProtects confidentiality; lost key means no recovery

Attack and defence pair

Attack: an attacker on the same wireless network runs a packet capture and reads a login form submitted over plain HTTP, recovering the password. Defence: force HTTPS with HSTS, encrypt the radio link with WPA3, and teach users to check the padlock. If the attacker still obtains the password, MFA means the stolen secret alone cannot open the account. Notice how the defences stack: encryption removes the easy win, MFA removes the payoff, and monitoring catches the attempt.

Worked example. A hospital laptop is stolen from a car. Because full-disk encryption is enabled, the thief sees only a login prompt; the patient records remain confidential. Because the device was also enrolled in a mobile-device-management system, it can be remotely wiped. If either control had been absent, the same theft would have become a reportable breach. This is why exam answers should name the control and the state of the data it protects.

Exam tip: "Confidentiality" on its own is a weak answer. Write "confidentiality — enforced with encryption at rest and in transit, plus role-based least privilege; broken by sniffing, disclosure, or stolen credentials." That sentence earns marks because it names the goal, a concrete control, and a concrete failure mode.
E2

Integrity and Non-Repudiation

Deep dive · trust

Integrity means data is complete, accurate, and has not been altered by an unauthorised party. Where confidentiality is about who can read, integrity is about whether the data can be trusted. A bank balance that has been quietly changed from 100 to 10,000 is not a confidentiality problem — the customer can see it perfectly well — it is an integrity problem. Detecting integrity loss requires a way to compare the current state with a known-good state.

The simplest tool is a hash function. A hash takes input of any length and produces a fixed-length digest; changing even one character changes the digest completely. SHA-256 is the common modern choice, while older algorithms such as MD5 and SHA-1 are deprecated because collisions have been demonstrated. A hash by itself proves nothing about identity, because an attacker who can change the file can also recompute the hash. To make the check trustworthy, combine the hash with a secret: a message authentication code (HMAC) uses a shared secret, and a digital signature uses the sender's private key so anyone with the public key can verify it.

Non-repudiation is the stronger promise that a party cannot deny having performed an action. Digital signatures provide it because only the holder of the private key could have produced the signature, assuming the key was not compromised. Audit trails provide a second form of non-repudiation: a tamper-evident log showing that a particular account, at a particular time, approved a transaction. In practice the two are combined. A signed contract plus a logged approval is much harder to repudiate than either alone.

MechanismDetects change?Proves who changed it?Typical use
Plain hash (SHA-256)YesNoChecksum for downloads and backups
HMACYesOnly to shared-secret holdersAPI message authentication
Digital signature (RSA/ECDSA)YesYes, publicly verifiableSoftware updates, contracts

Worked example. A software vendor publishes version 2.1 together with a SHA-256 digest signed with its private key. A user downloads the installer over a compromised mirror that swaps in a trojan. The installer still runs, but its digest no longer matches, and verification fails because the attacker cannot forge the vendor's signature. Here integrity protects users who never met the vendor, and non-repudiation lets the vendor prove it never signed the malicious build.

Attack and defence pair. An attacker performs a man-in-the-middle modification, rewriting a bank transfer recipient in transit. Defences: TLS to protect the channel, a signature or HMAC over the transaction, server-side validation that the recipient is on an approved list, and an out-of-band confirmation message. Each control is independent, so defeating one does not defeat the transaction.

Exam tip: If a question mentions "has the file been changed?", answer integrity and name hashing or signatures. If it mentions "can the sender deny it?", answer non-repudiation and name digital signatures plus audit logs. Confusing these two is the most common loss of easy marks in Module 1.
E3

Availability and Resilience

Deep dive · uptime

Availability is the property that authorised users can reach the system and data when they need them. It is often measured as a percentage of uptime: 99.9 percent allows roughly nine hours of downtime a year, while 99.99 percent allows under an hour. Every extra "nine" costs disproportionately more, so an availability target must be justified by business need rather than chosen for its own sake.

Threats to availability are unusually varied. Hardware fails, power fails, software has bugs, and links get cut. A denial-of-service attack deliberately exhausts a resource such as bandwidth, connection state, CPU, or disk space. Ransomware removes access by encrypting data. Even a well-intentioned change can take a service offline. Because the causes differ, the defences must be layered: redundancy removes single points of failure, backups allow recovery after data loss, load balancing spreads traffic, and capacity planning keeps headroom for spikes.

Two concepts students often mix up are redundancy and resilience. Redundancy means having more than one of something — two power supplies, two links, two servers. Resilience is the wider ability to keep delivering service when something breaks, which includes redundancy but also failover procedures, graceful degradation, and the human processes that restore service. A second pair is RAID, which protects against disk failure but not against deletion or ransomware, and backups, which protect against deletion but are useless if they have never been restored.

ThreatResource exhaustedPrimary controlSecondary control
Volumetric DDoSNetwork bandwidthUpstream scrubbing / anycast CDNRate limiting at the edge
SYN floodConnection tableSYN cookies, state limitsFirewall thresholds
RansomwareAccess to dataOffline, immutable backupsEDR and least privilege
Hardware failureServer / diskRAID, clustering, failoverMonitoring and spares

Worked example. An online shop expects a surge on a sale day. It puts a content-delivery network in front of the origin, uses autoscaling to add web servers, enables SYN cookies, and keeps database backups in a separate account. When a botnet floods the site, the CDN absorbs the volume, the origin never sees the traffic, and the sale goes ahead. The same design also survives a failed availability zone because the remaining zone takes over.

Exam tip: Availability answers should always mention backups that have been tested by restoring them. An untested backup is an assumption, not a control — a favourite distinction in fourteen-mark questions.
E4

Authentication, Authorisation, Accounting

Deep dive · AAA

AAA is the framework that turns a vague idea of "logging in" into three separable questions. Authentication asks "who are you?" and is answered with evidence: a password, a hardware token, a fingerprint, or a combination. Authorisation asks "what may you do?" and is answered by permissions attached to an account or role. Accounting asks "what did you do?" and is answered by logs. Keeping the three distinct matters because a failure in one is not a failure in the others: a valid user can be blocked by a permission error, and an unauthorised action can still be recorded perfectly.

Authentication evidence falls into three classic factors: something you know (password or PIN), something you have (smart card, token, phone), and something you are (biometric such as a fingerprint or iris). A fourth factor, somewhere you are, uses location as a condition. Multi-factor authentication combines two or more different factors; entering two passwords is not MFA because both are the same factor. App-based one-time codes or hardware keys are stronger than SMS, which is vulnerable to SIM-swap fraud. Biometrics are convenient but cannot be reset if compromised, so they are best paired with a device-bound key.

Authorisation makes its decision after identity is established. The safest pattern is least privilege with separation of duties, so no single person can both create a payee and approve the payment. Implicit deny means anything not explicitly allowed is refused, which is safer than allowing everything and then trying to list the exceptions. Accounting closes the loop: authentication and authorisation can be fooled, but well-protected logs let an investigator reconstruct what actually happened. That is why logs should be shipped off the host to a central system that the compromised account cannot edit.

StageQuestionExample controlCommon failure
AuthenticationWho are you?Password plus TOTP appReused or weak password
AuthorisationWhat may you do?RBAC, implicit denyExcessive rights, no review
AccountingWhat did you do?Central syslog / SIEMLocal logs deleted by attacker

Worked scenario. A contractor logs into a finance application. Authentication verifies a password and a hardware token. Authorisation allows read-only access to invoices but denies payment approval because their role lacks that permission. Accounting records the login, the queries, and the denied attempt. Later, an investigator uses those records to prove the contractor never had the ability to pay a fraudulent invoice. Each AAA stage contributes one piece of the evidence.

Exam tip: Students often write "authentication and authorisation are the same thing." They are not: authentication proves identity, authorisation grants rights. A three-mark question is usually won by stating that distinction in one sentence and giving an example of each.
E5

Risk Management, Quantified

Deep dive · decisions

Security is the management of risk, not its elimination. Risk is the possibility that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm. The working formula is risk = likelihood × impact. Likelihood asks how probable the event is, given exposure and attacker motivation; impact asks how bad the consequences would be, in money, time, reputation, or safety. An event that is certain but trivial and an event that is catastrophic but almost impossible may receive similar scores, which is why the two factors are written down separately before they are combined.

Qualitative assessment ranks risks as low, medium, or high using judgement and a simple matrix. It is fast and needs no reliable loss data, but different assessors may score the same risk differently. Quantitative assessment assigns numbers: single loss expectancy (SLE) is the cost of one occurrence, annual rate of occurrence (ARO) is how often it happens in a year, and annual loss expectancy (ALE = SLE × ARO) is the expected yearly cost. A control is economically justified when its annual cost is less than the reduction in ALE it delivers.

Once risks are ranked, there are four standard treatments. Avoid the risk by not doing the activity, reduce or mitigate it with controls, transfer it to someone else through insurance or outsourcing, or accept it formally because the cost of treatment exceeds the loss. Acceptance must be a decision by the business owner, recorded and reviewed, not a silent hope that nothing happens. Residual risk is what remains after controls are applied, and it is the number that management should be asked to accept.

Worked calculation

Risk: warehouse server room floods
SLE  = cost of hardware + rebuilding + lost orders = 40,000 currency units
ARO  = one flood every 10 years = 0.1
ALE  = SLE x ARO = 40,000 x 0.1 = 4,000 per year

Control: raised floor + leak sensor, installed and maintained = 900 per year
Residual ALE with control (ARO falls to 0.02) = 40,000 x 0.02 = 800
Benefit = 4,000 - 800 = 3,600 per year
Cost    = 900 per year  -> benefit exceeds cost, so the control is justified

E6

STRIDE and Threat Modelling

Deep dive · design

Threat modelling is the disciplined act of asking, before code is written, "how could this system be attacked?" STRIDE is a memorable six-part checklist. Spoofing violates authenticity by pretending to be another identity. Tampering violates integrity by altering data. Repudiation violates non-repudiation by denying an action. Information disclosure violates confidentiality. Denial of service violates availability. Elevation of privilege violates authorisation. The value of STRIDE is that it forces the reviewer to consider properties other than confidentiality, which is where most inexperienced designs are weak.

A threat model is usually built around a data-flow diagram. Draw the external entities, the processes, the data stores, and the trust boundaries between them, then walk each data flow and ask which STRIDE categories apply. An arrow that crosses a trust boundary deserves particular attention, because that is where the system stops trusting input. Each identified threat is recorded with its mitigation and an owner. An attack tree is a complementary view: the root is the attacker's goal, branches are alternative ways to reach it, and leaf nodes are the specific actions required.

Attack tree, worked. Goal: read another customer's invoice. Branch A — guess the identifier; leaves: enumerate sequential IDs, automate with a script. Branch B — steal a session; leaves: XSS, phishing, sniffing on HTTP. Branch C — abuse a function; leaves: force password reset, call an export API with a changed account number. The defender cuts whole branches at once: unguessable identifiers and per-object authorisation defeat A, HttpOnly cookies and HTTPS defeat most of B, and server-side checks plus out-of-band verification defeat C. Drawing the tree reveals that one control (per-object authorisation) sits on several paths and is therefore worth prioritising.

Exam tip: When asked to "apply a threat model," do not just list the six STRIDE letters. Apply each to a named component of the given system and propose a specific control; that is what converts a recall answer into an application answer.

Defense in Depth as an Architecture

Deep dive · layering

Defense in depth is the principle that a single control should never be relied on to protect an asset. It comes from military thinking: an attacker who breaches the outer wall should meet a second wall, then a guarded inner door, then a locked safe. In information security the layers are typically physical (locks, badges, guards), perimeter (firewall, DDoS scrubbing), network (segmentation, IDS/IPS), host (patching, EDR, hardening), application (input validation, authentication, authorisation), and data (encryption, backups, DLP). A seventh layer, people and process, cuts across all of them through training and policy.

Two ideas make the principle precise. Redundancy of control means two controls address the same failure, such as a firewall and a host firewall both blocking a port. Diversity of control means different kinds of defence cover the same attack, such as patching a vulnerable service and also placing it behind an authentication gateway; an attacker who defeats a patch by finding a zero-day still faces the gateway. Diversity is more valuable than duplication because it breaks the assumption that one class of defence will always work.

   +--------------------------------------------------+
   People + process wrap every layer: training, policy, IR plan

A breach of one layer is an incident, not a catastrophe, provided the next layer detects or stops the attacker. Logging must span all layers so the correlation engine can see the story.

Worked breach scenario. An attacker phishes a user and obtains a password. Layer 1 sees nothing, because the login comes from the internet like any other. Layer 2 flags an impossible-travel alert because the source country changed. Layer 3 would have blocked a known-bad binary, but the attacker used PowerShell. Layer 4 denies access to the finance role because the account is not a member. Layer 5 keeps the records encrypted, and the attempts are written to a central log. The user's mistake did not become a breach because four independent layers held. The correct exam conclusion is that defence in depth converts a single point of failure into a chain of checkpoints.

Exam tip: For a question asking "why is defence in depth important?", give the layered list, then a scenario in which one layer fails and the next one saves the asset. The scenario is what proves you understand the principle rather than just the phrase.
E8

The Hacker Landscape in Detail

Deep dive · actors

The "hat" labels are shorthand for intent and permission, not for skill. A white hat works with written authorisation and reports findings to the owner. A black hat acts without permission for profit, damage, or curiosity. A grey hat may find a flaw without permission and then disclose it, sometimes responsibly and sometimes not; the ethical problem is that unauthorised testing is still illegal even when the motive is good. A hacktivist seeks publicity for a cause, a script kiddie relies on tools they do not understand, a state-sponsored actor works patiently for espionage or sabotage, and an insider already holds legitimate access that they abuse.

Motivation predicts behaviour, which is useful for defence. Financially motivated criminals want the fastest path to money: ransomware, fraud, and credential theft. Espionage actors want to stay hidden for months, so they favour low-and-slow techniques and living off the land. Hacktivists want an audience, so defacement and data leaks are likely. Insiders know where the valuable data is and what "normal" looks like, which makes them the hardest to detect; defences must therefore include least privilege, user-behaviour monitoring, and mandatory vacation or rotation so that abuse surfaces when another person takes over.

The legal line is authorisation. In most jurisdictions, accessing a computer without permission, even without causing damage, is an offence; so is possessing tools intended for such access. Ethical hacking differs from criminal hacking not in the toolkit but in the scope, consent, and documentation surrounding its use. A professional keeps a signed rules-of-engagement document, limits testing to named systems, records every action, and reports through agreed channels. Without those, the same command that earned a bonus in a consultancy can earn a criminal record.

E9

Kill Chain and ATT&CK Thinking

Deep dive · phases

The classic kill chain breaks an intrusion into five steps: reconnaissance, scanning and enumeration, gaining access, maintaining access, and covering tracks. A widely used expansion adds weaponisation and delivery before exploitation, and installation and command-and-control after it. Both models make the same central point: an attacker must pass through a sequence of stages, and each stage produces observable evidence. Reconnaissance may appear as repeated lookups of your domain. Delivery appears as a malicious email. Exploitation appears as an unusual process. Installation appears as a new scheduled task. Command-and-control appears as regular outbound connections to a rare domain.

MITRE ATT&CK organises known adversary behaviour into tactics (the "why" of a step) and techniques (the "how"). Its tactics — reconnaissance, initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, defence evasion, credential access, lateral movement, collection, exfiltration, and impact — map closely onto the kill chain but are finer grained. Defenders use the framework to ask two questions: for each tactic, can we detect it, and can we prevent it? A gap in the matrix is a blind spot in the security programme.

The most important defensive idea is to shift left: detect as early in the chain as possible. Stopping a phishing email costs far less than rebuilding a domain after ransomware. Early detection also limits damage because the attacker has not yet established persistence. This is why reconnaissance telemetry, email filtering, and user reporting are worth as much as a sophisticated endpoint agent used only at the final stage.

Exam tip: If asked how the kill chain helps defence, the highest-scoring point is that each phase creates indicators and offers a chance to break the chain. Illustrate with one early control and one late control and compare their cost.
E10

Penetration Testing Practice

Deep dive · engagement

A penetration test is a time-boxed, authorised attack designed to answer a business question: can a competent adversary reach an asset that matters, and how? Unlike a vulnerability scan, which produces a list of possible issues, a pen test chains weaknesses into a demonstrated path and states the business consequence. A finding that says "SQL injection exists" is a scan result; a finding that says "SQL injection lets an external user export the full customer table and reset any password" is a pen-test result.

The engagement begins long before scanning. Scoping names the exact in-scope IPs, domains, applications, and third parties, and records exclusions such as production payment systems or real customer data. Rules of engagement fix the testing window, the test type (black, grey, or white box), the contacts for escalation, the handling of discovered credentials, and the agreed definition of "stop." Authorisation must be written and signed by someone empowered to grant it; verbal permission over the phone is not enough. Without these documents, a tester is simply an intruder.

Execution follows a loop of plan, discover, exploit, document, and retest. Findings are rated with CVSS, a standardised 0-to-10 severity score that combines the attack vector, complexity, privileges required, user interaction, and the impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Scores below 4.0 are low, 4.0 to 6.9 medium, 7.0 to 8.9 high, and 9.0 and above critical. Severity is not the same as business risk: a critical flaw on an isolated lab machine may matter less than a medium flaw on the public login page, so the report must discuss both the score and the exposure.

Test typeInformation givenStrengthWeakness
Black boxNoneRealistic outsider viewSlow; may miss internal flaws
Grey boxPartial, e.g. credentialsBalances realism and speedAssumes some access already
White boxFull, including sourceDeepest coverageLeast like a real attacker

A finding, written properly

Title:     Broken object-level authorisation on /api/invoice/{id}
Severity:  High (CVSS 8.1) - A01 Broken Access Control
Evidence:  GET /api/invoice/1042 as user A returns user B's invoice
Impact:    Any authenticated customer can read every other invoice
Root cause: Server trusts the id parameter without an ownership check
Fix:       Verify invoice.owner_id == session.user_id on every request
Retest:    Cross-account request now returns HTTP 403; closed 2026-02-14

Notice that the record includes evidence, impact, root cause, a specific fix, and a retest result. A report without the last two lines leaves the client with a problem but no closure.

Exam tip: Distinguish a vulnerability assessment from a penetration test in one sentence: the assessment finds and lists; the test exploits to prove impact and business consequence. Add that both require authorisation.
E11

Frameworks Compared

Deep dive · standards

Frameworks exist so that a test is repeatable and its results comparable. OSSTMM is a methodology manual organised around five channels — human, physical, wireless, telecommunications and data networks — and it emphasises measuring security rather than just listing findings. NIST SP 800-115 is a technical guide that describes planning, information gathering, scanning, exploitation, and reporting with an emphasis on minimising risk to the target during testing. OWASP concentrates on web application risk; its Top 10 is a prioritised awareness document rather than a full testing methodology. ISO/IEC 27001 is a certifiable management-system standard that requires risk assessment, controls, and continual improvement. MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base of adversary behaviour used to reason about detection coverage.

These are complementary, not competing. A professional engagement might use ISO 27001 to understand the organisation's control environment, NIST 800-115 to structure the test, OWASP to choose web test cases, ATT&CK to check detection coverage, and OSSTMM to ensure that physical and human channels are not forgotten. An exam answer that names the scope of each framework and says how they fit together demonstrates understanding, whereas a list of acronyms demonstrates only memorisation.

E12

What to Write for 3 Marks and for 14 Marks

Exam technique

Three-mark questions

Three marks usually means three distinct points, so write three short labelled sentences. "The CIA triad is confidentiality, integrity, and availability" earns at most one mark; add the definition and one control for each component. Use the structure term — meaning — control or failure mode. Never spend more than four or five lines; the marks are for breadth, not depth.

Fourteen-mark questions

A long question rewards structure. Spend the first two lines defining the key terms, then give a table or numbered list covering at least six points, then add one worked example with concrete numbers or a named technology, then a short discussion of limitations or the defender's view, and finish with a conclusion that answers the exact wording of the question. Include at least one diagram or preformatted sketch if the topic is architectural. Markers award marks for accurate terminology, so use the precise words: least privilege, non-repudiation, residual risk, defence in depth.

MarksExpected shapeApproximate lengthWhat loses marks
3Three defined terms with one example each4–6 linesWriting only the acronym
7Definition, list of six to eight points, one example½ pageNo example, mixed-up terms
14Definitions, table, worked example, defender view, conclusion1½–2 pagesEssay with no structure

Glossary to close the module

Threat — a possible cause of harm. Vulnerability — a weakness that a threat can use. Risk — the chance that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm. Control — a measure that reduces risk. Exploit — code or a technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability. Asset — anything of value that needs protection. Residual risk — what remains after controls are applied. Least privilege — the minimum access needed to do a job. Implicit deny — anything not explicitly allowed is refused. Non-repudiation — proof that an action occurred and who performed it.

Exam tip: Memorise the distinction between threat, vulnerability, and risk with the sentence "a threat uses a vulnerability to create risk." It appears in almost every paper in some form.
