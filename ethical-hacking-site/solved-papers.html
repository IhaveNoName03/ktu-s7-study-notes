Q1

Elements of information security

3 marks

List the elements of information security.

Answer

Information security (InfoSec) is the practice of protecting the confidentiality, integrity and availability of data and the systems that process it from unauthorised access, use, modification, disruption or destruction. It is conventionally described by the CIA triad, extended by several supporting properties.

  1. Confidentiality — only authorised parties may read the data. Mechanisms: encryption (AES at rest, TLS in transit), access-control lists and strong authentication.
  2. Integrity — data must not be altered without detection. Mechanisms: hashing (SHA-256), digital signatures, version control and write-once storage.
  3. Availability — systems and data must be usable when required. Mechanisms: RAID, clustering, backups, patching and DDoS protection.
  4. Authenticity and non-repudiation — prove the identity of the sender and prevent a party from denying an action, using digital certificates and signatures.
  5. Accountability and auditing — every action is traceable to a user through logs and audit trails.

Example: an internet-banking site uses TLS (confidentiality), stores passwords as salted hashes (integrity of identity), replicates its database across two data centres (availability), and records every login in an immutable audit log (accountability).

Exam tip: Begin with the CIA triad, then add authenticity, non-repudiation and accountability for full marks. One crisp definition plus one concrete mechanism per element is the expected 3-mark pattern.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: If a scenario is given (for example, an attacker defaces a website), classify it: defacement = integrity, data leak = confidentiality, site down = availability. Examiners reward this mapping.
Information security (InfoSec) is the practice and set of controls that protect data and information systems from unauthorised access, use, modification, disruption or destruction. Q2

Role of a penetration tester

3 marks

Define the role of a penetration tester.

Answer

A penetration tester (ethical hacker) is an authorised security professional who legally simulates real-world attacks against an organisation's systems to discover exploitable weaknesses before malicious actors do. The role is bounded by a signed rules-of-engagement document that defines scope, timing, permitted techniques and emergency contacts.

Key responsibilities and mechanism:

  • Perform reconnaissance and enumerate assets, then identify and validate vulnerabilities with manual checks and scanners (Nessus, Burp Suite, Metasploit).
  • Exploit confirmed weaknesses in a controlled way to demonstrate real business impact — never cause damage or exfiltrate real data.
  • Analyse risk as Threat × Vulnerability × Impact, rank findings, and produce a written report with evidence, remediation advice and a retest plan.
  • Maintain strict confidentiality, operate under legal authorisation (e.g. Computer Misuse Act / IT Act provisions), and log every action for auditing.

Example: a tester finds that a client's public web form is vulnerable to SQL injection, proves it by reading only a harmless test row, then reports the flaw with a parameterised-query fix; a malicious attacker would instead dump the entire customer table.

Pitfall: do not describe the tester as someone who "hacks for fun". The defining features are authorisation, scope limits and reporting.
Exam tip: 3 marks = definition + 3–4 roles + one example. Explicitly mention "authorised/legal" and "rules of engagement" — examiners look for those keywords.

Further exam angle

Pitfall: Do not present OWASP as a tool — it is a methodology/framework. Also distinguish the OWASP Testing Guide (process) from the OWASP Top 10 (risk list).
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) use the six numbered roles and the ethical-boundary note. For (b) draw the phase flow, name at least three Top 10 categories and list three "significance" benefits. Covering definition → procedure → significance is exactly how the 6 + 8 marks are awarded.
(a) Role of security and penetration testers (6 marks)

Definitions. A security tester evaluates the overall security posture of an organisation — policies, controls, configurations and awareness — to find weaknesses before attackers do. A penetration tester (pentester) goes further: with written authorisation they actively simulate real attacks against a defined scope to prove whether a vulnerability can actually be exploited and how far an attacker can get.

Q3

Two goals of social engineering

3 marks

What are two goals of social engineering?

Answer

Social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information, exploiting trust, fear, curiosity, authority or urgency rather than technical flaws. Its two principal goals are:

  1. To obtain sensitive information — credentials, PINs, account numbers, intellectual property or internal network details. Example: a phishing e-mail that impersonates the IT helpdesk and asks the victim to "verify" their password on a fake login page.
  2. To induce an unauthorised action or gain access — make the victim transfer money, install malware, open a malicious attachment or hold a door open (tailgating) so the attacker can physically enter a restricted area. Example: a pretext call from a fake "auditor" persuading an employee to disable a firewall rule.

Mechanism: the attacker gathers information about the target (footprinting), builds a believable pretext, establishes rapport or urgency, extracts the information or action, and then covers tracks. Common vectors include phishing, vishing, smishing, baiting, quid pro quo and piggybacking.

Exam tip: State the two goals as "get information" and "get an action/access", then give one distinct example for each. Add that humans are the weakest link to show understanding.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Define it as "hacking the human, not the machine". For the example, always show the chain: pretext → trust → information/access → misuse. One concrete example earns the mark; do not just list attack types.
Social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information. Instead of attacking software, the attacker exploits human traits such as trust, fear, curiosity, urgency, helpfulness and authority.

Mechanism: The attacker researches the target, adopts a credible pretext (a role or story), establishes trust or creates time pressure, extracts information or gains a physical/logical access, and then uses the obtained data for further attack. Common channels are phone (vishing), email (phishing), SMS (smishing), in person and social media.

Q4

Preventive measures for dumpster diving

3 marks

Give the preventive measures for dumpster diving.

Answer

Dumpster diving is a physical social-engineering attack in which an attacker retrieves sensitive information from discarded trash — paper documents, CDs, USB drives or old hardware. Prevention combines physical, procedural and technical controls.

  • Shred everything sensitive: use cross-cut (Level P-4 or higher) shredders for documents; shred or wipe CDs, DVDs and hard disks before disposal.
  • Secure disposal points: keep locked, access-controlled bins in a restricted, CCTV-monitored area; use a bonded, certified e-waste disposal vendor and demand a destruction certificate.
  • Policy and training: enforce a clean-desk/clean-bin policy, teach staff that even a name, badge or phone number is useful to an attacker, and forbid discarding media in public bins.
  • Physical and legal controls: fencing, lighting, guards and trespass signage; place bins off-site under supervision.

Example: a bank that cross-cut shreds statements, locks bins inside a guard-controlled loading bay and audits its disposal vendor defeats a diver who would otherwise reconstruct account numbers from torn printouts.

Exam tip: For 3 marks give three solid measures — shredding, locked/monitored bins, and policy/awareness — plus one line on why ("attackers reconstruct PII from trash").

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Classify it as low-tech but high-yield social engineering. Countermeasures worth one line each: cross-cut shredding, shred-all policies, locked "secure disposal" bins, media degaussing/wiping, and employee training. Note the legal wrinkle (trespass laws vary), which examiners like to see.

Definition. Dumpster diving is a physical social-engineering and information-gathering attack in which an attacker sifts through discarded material — trash bins, recycling, old media, printed records — to recover confidential information that has not been securely destroyed.

Mechanism and targets. The attacker looks for items that were thrown away rather than shredded: bank statements, invoices, printouts of credentials, sticky notes, old USB drives and hard disks, discarded laptops, packaging with barcodes and employee directories. Even "junk" yields names, usernames, account numbers and internal jargon that make later phishing or pretexting far more convincing. Attackers may pose as sanitation workers or simply collect bins put out on collection night.

Q5

Types of exploits with examples

3 marks

Illustrate the types of exploits with example

Answer

An exploit is a piece of code, input or technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability to cause an unauthorised, unintended or harmful result. Exploits are commonly classified as follows.

  1. Known (public) exploits — disclosed publicly and tracked by CVE numbers; weaponised in frameworks such as Metasploit or Exploit-DB. Example: EternalBlue (CVE-2017-0144) against unpatched SMBv1.
  2. Zero-day exploits — unknown to the vendor and for which no patch exists; highly valuable and dangerous. Example: the Stuxnet worm used several previously unknown Windows zero-days.
  3. Remote vs local exploits — remote exploits are delivered over a network without prior access (e.g. a remote buffer overflow in a service); local exploits require a foothold and elevate privileges (e.g. a kernel race condition).
  4. Application / web exploits — target logic flaws: SQL injection (' OR '1'='1), cross-site scripting, command injection, insecure deserialisation.
  5. Memory-corruption exploits — stack/heap overflows, use-after-free and format-string bugs that hijack execution flow.
  6. Client-side exploits — malicious files or web content that execute on the victim's machine (malicious PDF, drive-by download).
Exam tip: "Illustrate" demands examples, not just names. Give at least four categories each with a one-line example, and define zero-day clearly — it is the most frequently tested concept.

Further exam angle

Exam tip. Structure the answer as a two-way split — known vs zero-day and remote vs local — then list technique examples. Defining "exploit" versus "vulnerability" in the first line is a reliable mark.

Definition. An exploit is a piece of code or a technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability (a software or configuration flaw) to cause unintended behaviour — code execution, privilege gain or denial of service. The vulnerability is the weakness; the exploit is the means of using it.

Main types.

Q6

Detecting Man-in-the-Middle attacks

3 marks

How to detect Man in the Middle Attacks.

Answer

A Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attack secretly relays or alters communication between two parties who believe they are talking directly. Detection focuses on spotting the interception mechanism and its side effects.

  • ARP anomalies: duplicate IP-to-MAC mappings, frequent gratuitous ARP replies, or a gateway MAC that changes. Tools: arp -a, Arpwatch, XArp, netdiscover.
  • Certificate and TLS warnings: browser "invalid certificate" or hostname-mismatch alerts, unexpected downgrade from HTTPS to HTTP, or an untrusted CA in the chain. Defences: HSTS, HSTS preload and certificate pinning.
  • Traffic and timing anomalies: increased latency, retransmissions, duplicate packets or traffic flowing through an unexpected host, visible in Wireshark or an IDS/IPS (Snort/Suricata rules for ARP poisoning and DNS spoofing).
  • DNS anomalies: unexpected DNS answers or resolved IPs that do not match the legitimate host; DNSSEC validation can expose spoofing.

Example: if an employee's ARP cache suddenly maps the default gateway to another workstation's MAC address, an attacker is poisoning the cache to intercept traffic — Arpwatch would alert immediately.

Exam tip: Name two detection tools (Arpwatch/Wireshark/IDS) and two indicator types (ARP-table changes, certificate warnings). Finish with "use encryption (TLS/VPN)" as the preventive counterpart.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Distinguish passive MITM (only eavesdropping) from active MITM (modifying/injecting). Always pair the definition with one way the attacker gets into the middle (ARP/DNS/rogue AP) — that earns the mechanism mark.
A Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attack occurs when an attacker secretly inserts themselves into a communication channel between two parties who believe they are talking directly to each other. The attacker can listen to, capture, alter or inject the traffic, while both endpoints remain unaware.

Mechanism: The attacker must first position themselves on the path and then intercept the session. Common ways to achieve this position are ARP spoofing on a LAN, DNS spoofing/poisoning, rogue Wi-Fi access points (Evil Twin), HTTPS spoofing or malicious proxies. Once in the middle, the attacker relays messages between the victims to avoid suspicion while reading or modifying them — for example, capturing session cookies or changing the recipient's bank account number in a transaction.

Q7

Security challenges with routers

3 marks

Define the security challenges with routers.

Answer

Routers are the traffic directors of a network, and their compromise can expose or disable an entire organisation. The main security challenges are:

  • Weak credentials and defaults: factory usernames/passwords (admin/admin), Telnet instead of SSH, and default SNMP community strings (public/private) allow easy takeover and information leakage.
  • Unpatched firmware / IOS: known CVEs in the routing OS and services (e.g. SNMP, SSH, web admin) are exploited by bots and worms.
  • Misconfiguration: overly permissive ACLs, open management ports to the internet, no logging, and unused interfaces left up create attack surface.
  • Routing-protocol attacks: BGP hijacking, OSPF/IS-IS spoofing, route poisoning and RIP attacks can redirect or black-hole traffic.
  • Resource and physical attacks: CPU/memory exhaustion DoS, and physical tampering or console access if the device is not secured in a locked rack.

Example: an attacker who guesses the SNMP community string can read the router configuration, learn internal addressing and rewrite the routing table to send traffic through a malicious hop.

Exam tip: Cover default credentials, firmware patches, misconfiguration, routing-protocol abuse and physical security. Countermeasure keywords: SSH only, SNMPv3, ACLs, MD5-authenticated routing updates, and centralised logging.

Further exam angle

Pitfall: Non-repudiation is not the same as confidentiality — signatures prove who sent something but do not hide it; combine with encryption for both.
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) give six numbered challenges with one-line impacts. For (b) draw the sign/verify flow, name PKI + CA + digital signature + timestamp + audit log, and add the HMAC-vs-signature contrast for distinction. Always end on "accountability and dispute resolution."
(a) Security challenges faced by organizations today (6 marks)

Definition. Organisations today operate in a hybrid, cloud-connected, always-on threat landscape where data and identities cross boundaries constantly. Security challenges are the obstacles and risks that make protecting confidentiality, integrity and availability difficult in that environment.

Q8

Generations of firewall

3 marks

List the generations of firewall.

Answer

Firewall technology has evolved through five broad generations, each filtering at a higher level of the OSI model:

  1. First generation — packet-filtering firewalls: stateless ACLs that inspect source/destination IP, port and protocol at the network/transport layer. Fast but blind to application context and easily spoofed.
  2. Second generation — application-level / proxy gateways: terminate the connection and inspect the application payload (Layer 7); circuit-level gateways validate sessions at the session layer. Stronger inspection but slower.
  3. Third generation — stateful inspection firewalls: track the state of each connection in a state table and allow only packets belonging to established sessions — the standard enterprise firewall.
  4. Fourth generation — deep-packet inspection (DPI) / NGFW: inspect application-layer content, perform protocol analysis and integrate IPS; can identify applications regardless of port.
  5. Fifth generation — next-generation / cloud and AI-driven firewalls: combine NGFW with threat intelligence, sandboxing, TLS inspection, user identity and zero-trust / SaaS delivery (FWaaS).
Some textbooks merge the fourth and fifth generations or list only four (packet, application proxy, circuit-level, stateful). Mention both views if unsure — the sequence must always be from simple stateless filtering to deeper, stateful, application-aware inspection.
Exam tip: Learn the order with the key phrase "packet → proxy/circuit → stateful → DPI/NGFW → next-gen/cloud" and attach one OSI layer to each generation.

Further exam angle

Exam angle. Name the CIA property at stake, give one concrete tool or port, compare with the nearest related attack, and finish with detection and remediation controls.

Q9

Hiding evidence by altering logs

3 marks

How can we hide evidence by altering logs?

Answer

Log tampering is an anti-forensic technique in which an attacker modifies or removes the audit trail so an intrusion cannot be reconstructed. Common methods:

  • Deleting or clearing logs: emptying Windows Event Logs (wevtutil cl Security) or truncating /var/log/auth.log with shred/rm.
  • Editing or forging entries: inserting decoy events (log forging with encoded newlines) to bury the real activity or to frame another user.
  • Disabling logging: turning off audit policies, killing the syslog/auditd service, or redirecting output to /dev/null.
  • Timestomping: changing file MAC times (touch -r, Windows SetFileTime) so events appear outside the incident window.
  • Living off the land and encryption: using legitimate admin tools and encrypted tunnels so little is logged in the first place, plus wiping free space to destroy deleted records.

Defensive detection: forward logs in real time to a write-once, centralised SIEM on a separate host, synchronise clocks with NTP, monitor for gaps or service stops, and apply file-integrity monitoring to log directories.

Exam tip: The question is about how attackers hide evidence, but scoring answers add the defender's view — central, tamper-proof logging. Give three attack methods plus one detection method.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Name the goal (anti-forensics / destroying the audit trail), list one or two methods (delete, edit, timestamp change), and mention the key defence — centralised, write-once, time-synced log servers — for a complete 3-mark response.

Definition. Hiding evidence by altering event logs — often called log tampering, log scrubbing or anti-forensics — is the process of modifying, deleting or corrupting audit records so that an intruder's actions cannot be reconstructed. It is an anti-forensic technique aimed at defeating accountability and incident investigation.

Mechanism. After gaining access, an attacker edits or truncates log files (/var/log/*, Windows Event Logs), deletes specific event IDs, changes timestamps, or clears the entire log with utilities such as wevtutil cl. More subtle attacks remove only the attacker's own entries to avoid the suspicion a completely empty log would cause. Distributed or forwarded logs may be intercepted before they reach the collector.

Q10

Horizontal privilege escalation

3 marks

Define horizontal escalation technique.

Answer

Horizontal privilege escalation (horizontal movement) is an attack in which a user accesses the data or functions of another user who has the same privilege level, without elevating their own rights. It contrasts with vertical escalation, where a low-privilege user becomes an administrator or root.

Mechanism: the attacker first compromises one account — by password guessing, phishing, session-token theft or Pass-the-Hash — and then uses that legitimate identity to reach peers' resources: another user's mailbox, home directory or application records. On networks, "lateral movement" uses the same idea with tools such as PsExec, WMI or stolen Kerberos tickets to hop from one workstation to the next.

Example: an employee in accounts reads a colleague's payslip in the same HR application by changing the user-ID parameter from id=1042 to id=1043 — an Insecure Direct Object Reference (IDOR). No admin rights are gained, yet confidentiality is broken. Another example is using a harvested NTLM hash to log into a peer's workstation.

Countermeasures: enforce authorisation checks on every object, use unique accounts, MFA, network segmentation and EDR monitoring of lateral-tool activity.

Exam tip: The decisive phrase is "same privilege level, different user". Always contrast it in one line with vertical escalation to prove you know the difference.

Further exam angle

Exam tip. The defining wording is "lower privilege → higher privilege on the same system". Write the contrast with horizontal escalation in one sentence; examiners use it to separate full from partial answers.

Definition. Vertical privilege escalation (vertical escalation) is an attack in which a user with low privileges gains the higher privileges of an administrator, root or SYSTEM on the same system. It contrasts with horizontal escalation, where the attacker assumes the rights of another ordinary user at the same level.

Mechanism and common techniques.

OSSTMM modules & OWASP risks (Module I)

14 marks

a) Explain the four modules in OSSTMM. b) Describe the security risk reported using OWASP.

Answer

(a) The four modules of OSSTMM — 6 marks

OSSTMM (Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual) is a peer-reviewed, vendor-neutral methodology from ISECOM for testing operational security. It divides security into measurable control areas called modules; each module defines assets, indicators, test types, expected results and metrics so that findings are repeatable, auditable and expressed as residual risk rather than as a simple pass/fail.

  1. Information and Data Controls — governs how information is classified, handled, stored, transmitted and destroyed. Tests check data validation, privacy controls, documentation quality, and whether sensitive data leaks through error messages, logs or unsecured backups.
  2. Network Security Controls — covers perimeter and internal network devices: firewalls, routers, switches, IDS/IPS, VLANs and access-control lists. Tests look for open ports, weak segmentation, unauthenticated services, spoofing and sniffing exposure.
  3. Telecommunications Controls — protects voice and data circuits: PBX, VoIP, fax, modems, cabling and carrier links. Tests include wardialling for unauthorised modems, toll fraud, voicemail weaknesses and unprotected telephony protocols.
  4. Wireless Controls — covers RF systems: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, RFID, NFC and microwave links. Tests check for rogue access points, weak WPA2/WPA3 configuration, WPS flaws, bluesnarfing and unencrypted radio traffic.
ISECOM's full manual also defines Physical Security and Human Security controls. If the examiner expects six, present the two extras as extensions of the four core modules.

How testing works: for every module the tester (1) identifies assets and trust boundaries, (2) selects relevant tests, (3) applies passive then active tests under rules of engagement, (4) compares observations with the OSSTMM reference, and (5) reports Residual Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact.

(b) Security risks reported by OWASP — 8 marks

OWASP (Open Web Application Security Project) publishes the OWASP Top 10, a consensus list of the ten most critical web-application security risks, each mapped to CWE identifiers so teams can prioritise remediation. Attack mechanism and primary defence are summarised below.

Vulnerability assessment (VA) is a systematic, automated process of discovering, classifying and prioritising weaknesses in systems, applications and networks. Unlike a penetration test, VA stops at identification and recommendation; it does not deliberately exploit the flaw.

Answer

(a) Virus versus worm — 8 marks

A virus is malicious code that attaches itself to a legitimate host file or boot sector and spreads only when that host is executed or opened by a user. A worm is a standalone, self-replicating program that spreads across networks automatically, without attaching to a host file or requiring human action.

Mechanism & defence: viruses infect through executable/attachment or boot records; worms scan for vulnerable hosts and copy themselves. Defences: up-to-date AV/EDR, patching, disabling autorun/macros, least privilege, e-mail filtering and network segmentation.

(b) Denial-of-Service attacks and types — 6 marks

A DoS attack overwhelms a system, application or network link so legitimate users cannot be served. When the flood originates from many distributed compromised machines (a botnet), it is a DDoS.

  1. Volume/flood attacks: UDP, ICMP or SYN floods saturate bandwidth or connection tables. A SYN flood sends thousands of half-open TCP connections until the backlog is full.
  2. Amplification/reflection attacks: the attacker spoofs the victim's IP and sends small requests to open DNS/NTP/SSDP/Chargen servers that reply with much larger responses, multiplying traffic (e.g. DNS amplification up to ~50×).
  3. Protocol/state-exhaustion attacks: SYN flood, Ping of Death, teardrop and fragmented-packet attacks exploit protocol stack weaknesses.
  4. Application-layer attacks: HTTP GET/POST floods and Slowloris hold connections open with partial requests, exhausting web-server threads with low bandwidth.
  5. Distributed (DDoS): botnets of IoT/PCs generate traffic from many sources, making naive IP blocking ineffective.
Botnet (many zombies) ──► flooded packets ──► Victim server
   [PC][IoT][PC][IoT]        (SYN/UDP/HTTP)      CPU 100%
                                                  │
                Legitimate user ──► ✗ timeout ◄──┘
Defence: CDN/Anycast → rate-limit → SYN cookies → WAF/IPS → scrub centre

Countermeasures: SYN cookies, rate limiting, connection-timeouts, WAF, CDN/Anycast absorption, upstream blackholing, botnet traffic scrubbing, and an incident-response plan with the ISP.

Proxy vs packet firewalls & footprinting scans (Module II)

14 marks

a) Differentiate proxy and packet firewalling. b) Explain scanning method in footprinting and its types.

Answer

(a) Proxy versus packet-filtering firewall — 6 marks

A packet-filtering firewall inspects each packet's header (source/destination IP, port, protocol) against ACL rules at Layers 3–4 and forwards or drops it, usually without keeping connection state. A proxy (application-level) firewall terminates the client connection, inspects the application-layer payload at Layer 7, and opens a new connection to the server on the client's behalf, hiding the internal host.

BasisPacket filteringProxy firewall
OSI layerNetwork / transport (3–4)Application / session (5–7)
Inspection depthHeader only; stateless (or limited state)Full application payload and commands
SpeedFast, low latency, hardware-friendlySlower; per-connection processing overhead
SecurityWeak against spoofing and app-layer attacksStrong; can filter URLs, content and users
Address hidingNo — internal IPs may be exposedYes — acts as the visible endpoint
ExamplesACLs on routers, iptablesSquid, ISA/TMG, reverse proxies/WAFs

Defensive note: modern firewalls combine both — a packet filter provides speed, while a reverse proxy/WAF adds deep application inspection.

(b) Scanning in footprinting and its types — 8 marks

Scanning is the systematic probing of a target to discover live hosts, open ports, running services, operating systems and vulnerabilities — the bridge between passive footprinting and active exploitation. It turns a list of IP addresses into an attack surface map.

TCP three-way-handshake reference (used by scan types):

Scanner                          Target
   ── SYN ──────────────────────►
   ◄──────────────────── SYN/ACK   (port OPEN)
   ── ACK ──────────────────────►   (connection established)
   ◄──────────────────── RST       (port CLOSED)
  1. Port scanning: TCP connect scan completes the handshake (noisily); SYN stealth scan sends SYN and reads SYN/ACK without completing; FIN, NULL and XMAS scans send malformed flags — an RST means closed, silence means open (RFC-compliant stacks); ACK scanning maps firewall rules.
  2. Network scanning / host discovery: ICMP sweeps (nmap -sn), ARP sweeps and ping sweeps identify live hosts and topology.
  3. Service and version detection: banner grabbing and protocol probes reveal software and versions (nmap -sV).
  4. OS fingerprinting: analysis of TCP/IP stack responses and TTL/window values identifies the operating system (-O).
  5. Vulnerability scanning: tools (Nessus, OpenVAS) match discovered services against CVE signatures to list weaknesses.

Attack mechanism & countermeasures: scanning works by exploiting the target's predictable replies to crafted packets. Defenders use IDS/IPS signatures (e.g. Snort scan rules), firewall rules that drop unsolicited and malformed packets, port knocking, rate limiting, and log analysis for sequential-port or sweep patterns.

Defence: patch exposed services, strong perimeter filtering, IDS/IPS, least privilege and segmentation limit every class of remote attack.

(b) Brute-force attacks and their types — 8 marks

A brute-force attack submits every possible key, password or token until the correct one is found. The search space for a password of length L over an alphabet of size N is NL; the average attempt count is half that space, so weak, short passwords fall quickly at high guessing rates (offline hashes can be tested with GPUs at billions per second).

Method (numbered): (1) obtain the target — a login form, a captured hash or an encrypted file; (2) choose a wordlist/rule set and character space; (3) automate attempts with a tool (Hydra, John the Ripper, Hashcat); (4) detect success via a valid response, matching hash or decrypted content; (5) use the credential for access and record it.

Types:

  • Simple brute force — tries every character combination; exhaustive but slow.
  • Dictionary attack — tries a curated wordlist (rockyou.txt, common passwords) — much faster against human choices.
  • Hybrid attack — combines dictionary words with rules/mutations (Password1!, letmein123).
  • Rule-based / mask attack — uses known password structure (e.g. prefix + 4 digits).
  • Credential stuffing — replays username/password pairs leaked from one site on other sites.
  • Password spraying — tries one common password against many accounts to avoid lockouts.
  • Reverse brute force — one known password tried against many usernames.
  • Distributed brute force — spreads guesses across many machines to evade rate limits.

Countermeasures: long unique passwords/passphrases, salted slow hashes (bcrypt/Argon2), account lockout with exponential backoff, MFA, rate limiting, CAPTCHA and anomalous-login monitoring. Online guessing is stopped by throttling; offline guessing is slowed by salted adaptive hashes.

Network sniffers & SMTP confidentiality breach (Module III)

14 marks

a) Describe the types of network sniffers. b) With an example, How an attacker breaks the confidentiality using SMTP.

Answer

(a) Types of network sniffers — 6 marks

A sniffer (packet analyser) captures and decodes packets passing over a network interface. Sniffing is passive on a hub or a mirror port, and active when the attacker injects traffic (ARP poisoning) to redirect packets through itself.

  1. Packet capture / protocol analysers — Wireshark, tcpdump, Microsoft Network Monitor: capture raw frames and decode protocols layer by layer.
  2. Command-line / lightweight capture tools — tcpdump, tshark, netcap for quick, scriptable captures on servers.
  3. Wireless sniffers — Aircrack-ng, Kismet, Airodump-ng: capture 802.11 frames, discover hidden SSIDs and collect WPA handshakes.
  4. Active/ARP-poisoning sniffers — Ettercap, Cain and Abel, BetterCAP: poison ARP caches so the attacker becomes the man-in-the-middle and can sniff switched networks.
  5. Network/DSPAN/TAP-based sniffing — hardware test-access points or switch mirror ports used legitimately for monitoring, but abusable if access is gained.
  6. Application/specialised sniffers — password and session sniffers that focus on credentials, HTTP cookies or specific protocols.
Normal:      PC-A ──────── switch ──────── Server (B sees nothing)
Poisoned:    PC-A ──► Attacker ◄── Server   (B forwards & captures)
             ARP: "gateway = attacker's MAC"

Countermeasures: encryption (TLS/VPN/SSH), switched networks with port-security, dynamic ARP inspection, IDS detection of ARP anomalies, and anti-sniffer tools.

(b) Breaking confidentiality with SMTP — 8 marks

SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, TCP port 25/587) by default transmits messages and authentication in clear text. If an attacker can observe the mail flow, confidentiality is lost.

Attack mechanism, step by step:

  1. The attacker gains a vantage point on the path — a shared LAN, a compromised switch/router, a hostile Wi-Fi hotspot, or ARP poisoning that makes the attacker the default gateway.
  2. A sniffer (Wireshark/tcpdump) captures SMTP packets on port 25. Because there is no TLS, the entire session is readable.
  3. The attacker reads the SMTP dialogue: MAIL FROM, RCPT TO, subject line, and the full message body — confidential business data, OTPs or password-reset links are exposed.
  4. Authentication commands AUTH LOGIN/AUTH PLAIN carry base64-encoded credentials; base64 is encoding, not encryption, so the attacker decodes it and recovers the username and password.
  5. With those credentials the attacker logs in, reads stored mail, sends spoofed messages or relays spam (open-relay abuse), and can modify mail in transit — an integrity breach as well.

Worked example: on a campus LAN, a student runs BetterCAP to poison the ARP cache of a colleague and the mail server. Wireshark shows AUTH LOGIN with a base64 string that decodes to "alice:Summer2023". The attacker then reads Alice's inbox and intercepts a password-reset link before she does.

Countermeasures: force STARTTLS / implicit TLS on submission port 587 and server-to-server 25; use certificate validation; enforce SPF, DKIM and DMARC against spoofing; enable MFA; segment the network, deploy dynamic ARP inspection, and use VPNs on untrusted networks. End-to-end PGP/S/MIME protects content even from a compromised server.

Exam tip: For (a) name at least four sniffer types with examples and note passive vs active. For (b) stress that base64 is not encryption, walk through the capture-to-decode steps, and finish with STARTTLS/SPF-DKIM-DMARC/MFA — those are the high-scoring points.

A web server is software (Apache HTTP Server, Nginx, IIS) on a host that serves web resources to clients over HTTP/HTTPS using the client–server model.

  1. DNS resolution: the browser resolves www.example.com to the server's IP via DNS.
  2. Connection: the client opens a TCP connection to port 80 (HTTP) or 443 (HTTPS), completing the three-way handshake; TLS negotiates a session for HTTPS.

14 marks

Answer

(a) Working of Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) — 6 marks

Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) is an injection flaw in which an attacker places malicious JavaScript into a trusted web page; the victim's browser executes it with the site's origin privileges, so it can steal cookies, session tokens or credentials, deface pages or redirect the user.

Mechanism (numbered): (1) the attacker finds an input field, URL parameter or comment that reflects or stores user input without encoding; (2) they inject a script payload such as <script>alert(document.cookie)</script>; (3) a victim loads the page, and the browser runs the script as if it came from the legitimate site; (4) the script sends the stolen data to the attacker's server.

Types:

  • Reflected (non-persistent): payload is in the request URL and echoed in the response — victim must click a crafted link.
  • Stored (persistent): payload is saved in the database (comment, profile) and hits every visitor — the most dangerous form.
  • DOM-based: the flaw is in client-side JavaScript that writes untrusted data into the DOM; the payload never reaches the server.
Attacker crafts link ─► Victim clicks ─► Vulnerable site reflects payload
        ▲                                   │
        └──── stolen cookie/session ◄── script runs in victim's browser

Countermeasures: context-aware output encoding (HTML, attribute, URL, JS), input validation, Content Security Policy, HttpOnly/Secure/SameSite cookies, and safe DOM APIs. Never rely on blacklisting <script> alone.

(b) When a mobile phone is hacked — effects and prevention — 8 marks

A compromised smartphone gives an attacker access to a rich store of personal data and powerful sensors. What happens:

  • Data and credential theft: contacts, photos, SMS/OTPs, e-mail, saved passwords and banking credentials are exfiltrated; 2FA SMS codes can be read in real time.
  • Financial fraud and identity theft: banking/UPI apps are abused, payments initiated, and accounts opened in the victim's name.
  • Privacy invasion: the camera, microphone, GPS and call logs are tapped (spyware/stalkerware); location tracking enables physical harm.
  • Malware impact: ransomware encrypts files, adware floods the device, and the phone joins a botnet to send spam or mine cryptocurrency; battery and data usage spike.
  • Persistence and lateral spread: the device is used as a foothold to attack contacts via SMS worms and to compromise cloud accounts.

Q19

Hiding files & privilege escalation (Module V)

14 marks

a) Describe the five primary methods used in hiding files. b) Explain the process of privilege escalation with figure.

Answer

(a) Five primary methods of hiding files — 6 marks

Attackers hide tools, payloads and stolen data on a compromised host so they survive reboot and evade casual inspection. The five classic methods are:

  1. Hidden / system file attributes — setting the hidden and system bits so files disappear in normal directory listings: attrib +h +s payload.exe on Windows, or a leading dot (.config) on Linux.
  2. NTFS Alternate Data Streams (ADS) — data is appended to an existing file via a named stream (type secret.txt > readme.txt:hidden) where ordinary tools and Explorer show no extra file; only Streams or Sysinternals tools reveal it.
  3. Steganography — concealing data inside the bits of images, audio, video or documents (least-significant-bit substitution) so the carrier looks normal; tools include Steghide and Stegano.
  4. Rootkits — kernel- or user-level hooks that intercept system calls and actively hide files, processes and network connections from the operating system and security tools.
  5. Encryption / password-protected containers — data is stored in encrypted archives or virtual containers (VeraCrypt, BitLocker) so even if found its contents cannot be read without the key.

Related tricks include storing data in slack space, bad clusters or the registry, and fileless (in-memory) payloads. Defence: enable "show hidden files", scan with ADS-aware tools and EDR, hash-monitor critical directories, and use behaviour-based detection for rootkits.

(b) Privilege escalation process with figure — 8 marks

Privilege escalation is the act of moving from a lower to a higher access level. Vertical escalation raises privileges (user → administrator/SYSTEM/root); horizontal escalation moves to a peer account at the same level.

Process (numbered):

  1. Gain initial foothold — obtain a low-privilege shell through phishing, a client-side exploit or a stolen credential.
  2. Enumerate the system — whoami, id, OS/version, installed patches, running services, scheduled tasks, SUID/GUID files, sudo rules and saved credentials.
  3. Find a weakness — an unpatched kernel CVE, a misconfigured service running as SYSTEM, weak service permissions, DLL hijacking, a plaintext password in a config file, or a token impersonation opportunity.
  4. Exploit it — run the matching local exploit or abuse the misconfiguration to execute code in a higher-privilege context.
  5. Verify and maintain access — confirm with whoami /priv, add an admin account, install a persistent backdoor and then clear logs to cover tracks.
    ┌──────────────── PRIVILEGE LADDER ────────────────┐
    │                                                  │
 [Guest / User]  ──vertical exploit──► [Administrator]  │
    │                                        │         │
    │ horizontal (peer)                       ▼         │
    ▼                                   [SYSTEM / root]◄┘
 [Another user]                          full control
   (same level)                                ▲

                                     / token theft / SUID abuse

Attack vs defence: attackers rely on missing patches and excessive rights; defenders apply least privilege, remove local admin rights, patch promptly, enable UAC/ASLR/DEP, audit sudo/admin groups, use EDR behaviour monitoring and separate privileged accounts.

Q20

System hacking & hiding evidence on a network (Module V)

14 marks

a) Explain about system hacking. b) Describe the methods used in hiding evidence on network.

Answer

(a) System hacking — 8 marks

System hacking is the structured process of compromising a computer system — gaining unauthorised access, escalating control, maintaining that access and concealing the intrusion. Ethical hackers follow the same phases as attackers but under authorisation, and document every step.

  1. Reconnaissance / information gathering — collect target details passively (footprinting, search engines, DNS) and actively (ping, port scans, banner grabbing).
  2. Enumeration — extract concrete identifiers: user and group names, shares, services, OS versions and network resources (netstat, nbstat, LDAP queries).
  3. Vulnerability analysis — map discovered services to known weaknesses using scanners and CVE databases.
  4. Gaining access — exploit a vulnerability or crack passwords (dictionary/brute-force, rainbow tables, sniffing) to obtain a shell.
  5. Escalating privileges — move from a standard account to administrator/SYSTEM/root using local exploits, weak services or stolen tokens.
  6. Maintaining access — install backdoors, rootkits, binding ports, scheduled tasks or rogue accounts so access survives reboot.
  7. Covering tracks — clear or forge logs, hide files, timestomp, and erase toolkits.

(b) Methods of hiding evidence on a network — 8 marks

Network anti-forensics aims to remove or confuse the traces an investigator would use to reconstruct an attack: flow records, logs, packet captures and device state.

  1. Deleting or altering logs — clearing local event/syslog files, editing entries, or flooding logs with forgeries so the real events are lost in noise (log forging).

  5. Steganography in network data — hiding payloads in protocol headers, image files or covert timing channels that look like normal traffic.
  6. Timestomping and log-tampering correlation attacks — altering timestamps and deleting dependent records so timelines no longer align.

Q1

Steps in penetration testing

3 marks

List the steps involved in penetration testing.

Answer

Definition. Penetration testing (pen testing) is an authorised, simulated attack on a computer system, network or application, performed to discover security weaknesses before a real attacker does. Unlike a vulnerability scan, it actively exploits the weaknesses found.

The steps (PTES / NIST SP 800-115).

  1. Pre-engagement and scoping. Agree objectives, assets in scope, rules of engagement, timing, and get written authorisation. This is the legal boundary of the test.
  2. Intelligence gathering (footprinting). Collect information about the target — IPs, domains, employees, technologies, public records.
  3. Threat modelling. Identify likely attackers, their motives and the attack paths they would use.
  4. Vulnerability analysis. Scan and enumerate to find exploitable flaws; rank them by severity.
  5. Exploitation. Attempt to break in using the discovered flaws, exactly as an attacker would, but within scope.
  6. Post-exploitation. Determine the impact — what data or privileges were reachable, whether access can be maintained, and how deep the compromise goes.
  7. Reporting and remediation. Document findings, risk ratings, evidence and prioritised fixes; re-test after remediation.

Example. A tester scopes a web server, runs a port scan (finding port 80/443 open), discovers an unpatched SQL injection flaw, exploits it to read a test record, then reports it with a patch recommendation.

Q2

Dimensions of vulnerability assessment

3 marks

Give the three dimensions in vulnerability assessment.

Answer

Definition. Vulnerability assessment (VA) is the systematic process of discovering, classifying and prioritising weaknesses in a system, network or application. It answers three questions: what assets do we have, what is wrong with them, and how much does each weakness matter?

The three dimensions.

  1. Asset / scope dimension (discovery). Establish what exists and what is exposed — hosts, IPs, ports, services, operating systems and applications. This is the attack surface; you cannot assess what you have not inventoried.
  2. Vulnerability / threat dimension (detection). Probe each asset for known weaknesses — missing patches, default credentials, misconfigurations, weak ciphers — and match findings to references such as CVE/NVD IDs with a scanner (Nessus, OpenVAS).
  3. Risk / impact dimension (prioritisation). Judge each finding by severity, likelihood of exploitation and business impact, then rank it (critical / high / medium / low) so remediation effort is spent where it matters most.

Example. Discovery finds 50 hosts; scanning finds an unpatched Remote Code Execution (RCE) flaw on one internet-facing server; risk analysis rates it "critical" because it is externally reachable and leads to full system compromise, so it is fixed first.

Equivalent framing you may see in the syllabus: VA is also described by where it is done — network-based, host-based and application-based scanning — or as the three phases pre-assessment, assessment and post-assessment. All describe the same discover → detect → prioritise idea.

Q3

Social engineering attack cycle

3 marks

Define the steps for the social engineering attack cycle.

Answer

Definition. Social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information. The attacker exploits human trust, curiosity, fear or helpfulness instead of a technical flaw. The attack cycle is the sequence of stages the attacker moves through.

  1. Target selection. Choose the victim — a person, department or role (e.g. HR, helpdesk, finance) likely to hold the desired information or access.
  2. Information gathering / footprinting. Research the target using public sources, social media, job ads, company website and prior breaches, so the approach sounds credible.
  3. Building trust / pretexting. Establish a believable identity and rapport — pretend to be IT support, a vendor, a delivery person or a senior manager, often creating urgency.
  4. Exploiting the trust. Make the request that triggers the disclosure — ask for a password, a favour, a door to be held, an attachment to be opened or a wire transfer.
  5. Executing the attack. The victim complies; the attacker obtains the information, credentials or physical access and uses it to enter the system or premises.
  6. Covering tracks / exiting. Remove evidence, return to a normal demeanour and withdraw, leaving the victim unaware so the access can be reused.

Example. An attacker learns the helpdesk manager's name from LinkedIn, calls claiming to be "IT, resetting your account", creates urgency about a locked mailbox, and obtains the password — then logs in and clears the mail logs.

Exam tip. Memorise the six stages in order: select → research → build trust → exploit → execute → cover tracks. Most marking schemes give one mark per stage, so exact ordering earns marks quickly.

Q4

Preventing shoulder surfing

3 marks

What are the protective measures taken to avoid shoulder surfing?

Answer

Definition. Shoulder surfing is a physical observation attack in which a nearby person watches the victim's screen or keyboard — over the shoulder, through a window or via a camera — to capture passwords, PINs or sensitive data. It is a low-tech but highly effective form of information disclosure.

Protective measures.

  • Privacy filters: fit a restricted-viewing-angle screen protector so the display is legible only from directly in front.
  • Situational awareness: check surroundings before entering credentials; avoid working on confidential data in trains, cafés, lifts or open plan areas; sit with your back to a wall.
  • Mask input: obscure password fields with asterisks/dots, use on-screen randomised keypads for PINs, and never write passwords on notes or screens.
  • Physical access control: restrict entry with badges/mantraps, position screens away from windows and corridors, and lock the workstation (Win+L) whenever leaving the desk.
  • Stronger authentication: use biometrics, smart cards, hardware tokens or MFA so that even an observed password is insufficient for access.
  • Policy and training: teach staff to challenge strangers and to report suspicious loitering; apply clean-desk and clear-screen policies.

Example. At an ATM, a person standing too close watches a PIN being typed. A privacy shield over the keypad, a quick glance behind and covering the keypad with the other hand defeat the attack.

Exam tip. Contrast with piggybacking (following someone through a door) — a classic trap. Shoulder surfing = looking; piggybacking = following. Write "privacy screen filter" first; it is the most commonly credited control.

Q5

Types of network sniffing

3 marks

Illustrate the types of network sniffing.

Answer

Definition. Network sniffing (packet capture) is the interception and analysis of data packets as they cross a network. A sniffer places a network interface in promiscuous mode so it receives all frames on its segment, then a protocol analyser (Wireshark, tcpdump) decodes them.

Type 1 — Passive sniffing. Works on shared-media networks (hubs, early Ethernet, wireless in promiscuous mode) where every host sees all traffic. The attacker simply listens; no packets are injected, so it is almost undetectable. Example: capturing FTP credentials on a hub-based LAN.

Type 2 — Active sniffing. Required on switched networks, where traffic is destined only for the intended port. The attacker must manipulate the network to redirect traffic to itself, using techniques such as:

  • ARP spoofing / poisoning: send forged ARP replies to associate the attacker's MAC with the gateway's IP.
  • MAC flooding: flood the switch CAM table so it fails open and broadcasts frames to all ports.
  • DHCP spoofing: hand out the attacker's machine as the default gateway or DNS server.
  • DNS spoofing and session hijacking: redirect names or steal session tokens after interception.

Q6

Types of exploits

3 marks

Illustrate the types of exploits.

Answer

Definition. An exploit is a piece of code or a technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability (a software or configuration flaw) to cause unintended behaviour — code execution, privilege gain or denial of service. The vulnerability is the weakness; the exploit is the means of using it.

Main types.

  1. Known (public) exploits: published against identified CVEs, available in Exploit-DB, Metasploit modules or vendor advisories. Patched systems are immune once updated.
  2. Zero-day exploits: target a flaw unknown to the vendor and for which no patch exists — the most dangerous category.
  3. Remote exploits: run over the network against a service (e.g. a buffer overflow in an open port) without prior access.
  4. Local exploits: require local access first and are used for privilege escalation (e.g. a vulnerable setuid binary).
  5. Client-side exploits: target the client — malicious web pages, documents or email attachments exploiting browser/office flaws.
  6. By technique: buffer/heap overflow, SQL injection, format-string, race condition, use-after-free, cross-site scripting, deserialisation and credential attacks.

Example. An unpatched web server has an SQL injection flaw; an attacker sends a crafted query (a known remote exploit) and dumps the user table. A zero-day in a browser plug-in would be a client-side exploit with no available patch.

Delivery note. Exploit kits bundle several browser exploits and serve them from a compromised site; "exploit chaining" combines several minor flaws to achieve a serious impact.

Q9

Examining encrypted files

3 marks

How can we examine encrypted files?

Answer

Definition. Examining an encrypted file means recovering its plaintext contents (or at least useful information about it) without necessarily possessing the authorised decryption key. The approach depends on whether the key can be found, guessed or bypassed.

Methods, from easiest to hardest.

  1. Locate the legitimate key / password. Search for stored keys, configuration files, browser-saved passwords and written notes; ask the owner; check key rings and password managers.
  2. Crack the password. If the file is password-protected, use dictionary, rule-based or brute-force attacks with tools such as John the Ripper, Hashcat or the archive/PDF cracker for the specific format. GPU acceleration and wordlists make this practical for weak passwords.
  3. Capture the key in use. Keyloggers, memory (RAM) scraping, cold-boot attacks and API hooking can recover a key while the file is legitimately open — after it is closed, the key often vanishes from memory.
  4. Exploit weak key management or implementation. Predictable random number generators, hard-coded keys, ECB mode patterns and known-plaintext attacks can reveal data without brute force.
  5. Cryptanalysis / known algorithms. Legacy ciphers (DES, WEP, weak RC4) may be broken directly; strong ciphers (AES-256) are infeasible to break by brute force.
  6. Examine without decrypting. Analyse the file header/signature, size, entropy (high entropy suggests encryption), timestamps and metadata to identify the algorithm and container, and carve copies of the encrypted file for later attack.

Example. A ZIP archive is password protected; a dictionary attack recovers a weak password, after which the extracted files can be read normally.

Q10

Vertical privilege escalation

3 marks

Define vertical escalation technique.

Answer

Definition. Vertical privilege escalation (vertical escalation) is an attack in which a user with low privileges gains the higher privileges of an administrator, root or SYSTEM on the same system. It contrasts with horizontal escalation, where the attacker assumes the rights of another ordinary user at the same level.

Mechanism and common techniques.

  • Vulnerable setuid/setgid binaries or kernel flaws: a program running with elevated rights is tricked into executing attacker code, granting a root shell.
  • Stolen or cracked credentials: keyloggers, memory dumping (e.g. Mimikatz) or password reuse reveal an administrator's password or hash, which is then used to log in as admin.
  • Misconfigured services and permissions: writable service binaries, unquoted service paths, weak registry ACLs or scheduled tasks let a normal user replace an executable that later runs as SYSTEM.
  • Token impersonation / DLL hijacking / injection: the attacker inherits a privileged token or forces a privileged process to load a malicious DLL.

Typical steps. Enumerate current privileges and OS version → find a vulnerable component → run the exploit → obtain a higher-privilege shell → disable auditing and maintain access.

Example. A standard user exploits an unpatched local kernel vulnerability to add themselves to the Administrators group, then reads password hashes they were previously denied access to.

Countermeasures: least privilege, prompt patching, UAC, strong admin credentials, application control and privilege-monitoring/EDR.

Exam tip. The defining wording is "lower privilege → higher privilege on the same system". Write the contrast with horizontal escalation in one sentence; examiners use it to separate full from partial answers.

Further exam angle

Horizontal privilege escalation (horizontal movement) is an attack in which a user accesses the data or functions of another user who has the same privilege level, without elevating their own rights. It contrasts with vertical escalation, where a low-privilege user becomes an administrator or root.

Mechanism: the attacker first compromises one account — by password guessing, phishing, session-token theft or Pass-the-Hash — and then uses that legitimate identity to reach peers' resources: another user's mailbox, home directory or application records. (b) Effects of hacking on society — 6 marks

Hacking is not a victimless technical event; its consequences spread to individuals, organisations and nations.

  1. Financial loss: theft of funds, card fraud, ransomware payments, downtime and the high cost of investigation, remediation and legal compliance.
  2. Loss of privacy and identity theft: stolen personal data is used for impersonation, fraudulent loans and long-term reputational damage.
  3. Disruption of critical services: attacks on hospitals, banks, power grids, transport or schools endanger safety and daily life.

Q13

Footprinting and piggybacking

14 marks

(a) Explain footprinting and its types. (b) Describe piggybacking.

Answer

(a) Footprinting and its types — 8 marks

Definition. Footprinting (reconnaissance) is the first phase of an attack in which the attacker collects as much information as possible about a target — network, systems, people and organisation — before attacking. The goal is to build a detailed map that reveals the easiest entry point while remaining, where possible, undetected.

Types.

TypeHow it worksExamples / tools
Passive footprintingNo direct contact with the target; uses public sources, so virtually undetectableGoogle dorks (site:, filetype:), WHOIS, DNS records, social media, job ads, public filings, Shodan, theharvester
Active footprintingDirect probes that may be logged but yield precise technical detailping, traceroute, port scans (Nmap), banner grabbing, OS fingerprinting, SNMP queries, DNS zone transfer attempts
Network footprintingMaps IP ranges, domains, subnets, ports, services and firewallsping sweep, Nmap, netcat, traceroute

Exam tip. Lead with the active/passive split (the highest-weighted distinction), add at least three named tools, and always append a defence line — footprinting questions routinely ask for countermeasures implicitly.

(b) Piggybacking — 6 marks

Definition. Piggybacking is a physical social-engineering attack in which an unauthorised person gains entry to a restricted area by following closely behind an authorised person through a door, gate or turnstile. It exploits politeness or inattention rather than a technical flaw.

How it works (procedure).

  1. The attacker observes a secure entrance and waits near it, often dressed as staff, a delivery worker or carrying papers.
  2. An authorised employee approaches and badges in or holds the door open.
  3. The attacker slips in behind them before the door closes — sometimes by asking "could you hold that?" (piggybacking with consent) or simply slipping in without consent (commonly called tailgating).
  4. Once inside, the attacker can steal equipment, plant a rogue device, plug into the internal network or access documents.
   [Authorised employee] --badge--> (DOOR) --> secure area
                                      ^
   [Attacker] -------------------------|  follows behind,
        (no badge)                        door held open

Example. A person carrying a parcel asks an employee to hold the server-room door; the employee obliges, and the attacker walks in and connects a malicious Raspberry Pi to the switch.

Countermeasures: mantraps/vestibules with two doors, turnstiles, anti-passback badge systems, security guards, never holding doors for strangers, visitor logs and CCTV.

Exam tip. Distinguish piggybacking (access by following) from shoulder surfing (data by looking), and mention tailgating as the no-consent variant. Name mantraps and anti-passback as the top defences.

Answer

(a) Compare Trojans and backdoors — 8 marks

Definitions. A Trojan horse is malware that disguises itself as legitimate, useful software to trick a user into installing it, while secretly performing a malicious function. A backdoor is a hidden entry point into a system that bypasses normal authentication, allowing an attacker to return unnoticed. A Trojan is often the delivery vehicle; a backdoor is often the capability it installs.

Exam tip. For "compare", give a table of at least five rows and close with one conclusion sentence. The Trojan = disguise/delivery and backdoor = hidden access distinction is the key marking point.

(b) Enumeration and its categories — 6 marks

Definition. Enumeration is the systematic extraction of detailed information — user names, groups, shares, services, network resources and policies — from a target, usually after scanning has found live hosts and open ports. Unlike passive footprinting, it actively queries services and is more invasive and detectable.

  1. Network / domain enumeration: DNS records and zone transfers, NetBIOS names, SNMP community strings, routing tables. Tools: nbtstat, snmpwalk, dig, nslookup.
  2. System / OS enumeration: identify OS, version, patches, hostname and environment through banners and probes.
  3. User and group enumeration: list accounts, group membership and password policy — via SMB, LDAP, RPC or default accounts. Tools: enum4linux, ldapsearch.
  4. Share / resource enumeration: discover file and printer shares, hidden shares (net view, smbclient) and their permissions.
  5. Service / application enumeration: identify running services, versions and banners on open ports (e.g. FTP, SMTP, IIS, SQL).
  6. Vulnerability enumeration: correlate the collected versions and accounts with known CVEs to plan exploitation.

Example. snmpwalk with the default community "public" returns the full user list and interfaces of a router, giving the attacker valid account names for a password attack.

Countermeasures: disable unnecessary services and SNMP or change default communities, restrict NULL sessions, enforce strong authentication and ACLs, patch, and monitor logs for enumeration patterns.

Exam tip. Define enumeration as "active, detailed discovery after scanning", list the categories with one tool each, and add the countermeasure line — examiners look for the scanning → enumeration → exploitation progression.

(b) Manipulating DNS records using DHCP spoofing — 6 marks

Definition. DHCP spoofing is an attack in which a rogue DHCP server responds to clients' DHCP requests faster than the legitimate server, supplying false network configuration. Option 3 (default gateway) and option 6 (DNS server) are the two fields manipulated.

  1. The attacker connects a rogue DHCP server to the victim's broadcast domain and listens for DHCPDISCOVER broadcasts.
  2. The rogue server replies with an offer whose default gateway (option 3) is the attacker's IP — so all victim traffic is routed through the attacker.
  3. The same offer sets DNS server (option 6) to a DNS server controlled by the attacker.
  4. The victim's DNS lookups now go to the attacker, who returns forged A records — e.g. mapping www.bank.com to a fake website IP.
  5. The victim connects to the fake site over what they believe is a normal connection, and the attacker harvests credentials (and can relay traffic to the real site to avoid suspicion).
  [Victim] --DHCP request--> (broadcast)
                 |               |
        legit DHCP (slow)     rogue DHCP (fast)
                               | option 3 = attacker IP
                               | option 6 = attacker DNS
                 v
  [Victim] --DNS query for bank.com--> [Attacker DNS]
                 <-- forged A record: fake-site-IP --
  [Victim] =====> [Fake bank site]  (credentials stolen)

Impact: credential theft, malware delivery, complete traffic interception and denial of service if a bogus gateway is supplied. Countermeasures: DHCP snooping on switches (trust only the legitimate DHCP port), Dynamic ARP Inspection, DNSSEC validation, 802.1X port authentication, and monitoring for unauthorised DHCP servers.

Exam tip. Cite option 3 and option 6 explicitly — that shows real understanding. Draw the flow diagram with "rogue DHCP faster than legit", forged A record and fake site, then list the three defences.

Exam tip. Name at least six sources and explain the relationship: CVE gives the ID, NVD/CVSS give the score, CWE gives the class, vendor advisories give the patch.

(b) Working of session hijacking — 6 marks

Definition. Session hijacking (session spoofing) is the takeover of a legitimate user's active session by stealing or predicting the session identifier (session ID / cookie), allowing the attacker to act as that user without knowing the password.

  1. Obtain the session ID. Methods: packet sniffing over unencrypted HTTP (sidejacking on public Wi-Fi), XSS that reads document.cookie, MITM/proxy interception, prediction of weak sequential tokens, or session fixation (forcing the victim to use a token the attacker knows).
  2. Keep the session alive. The attacker avoids logging out and may refresh the token to prevent expiry.
  3. Replay the token. The attacker sends a request with the stolen cookie/ID to the server.
  4. Gain access. The server treats the attacker as the authenticated victim — data is read or actions are performed with the victim's privileges.
  1) Victim --login--> Web server   <-- sets cookie SID=abc123
  2) Attacker sniffs / steals SID=abc123
  3) Attacker --request + SID=abc123--> Web server
  4) Server: "valid session" --> attacker is the victim

Types: active (attacker injects commands and takes over) and passive (attacker silently observes the session). Countermeasures: enforce HTTPS/HSTS, set cookies Secure + HttpOnly + SameSite, regenerate the session ID after login, use long random tokens, short timeouts and logout, bind sessions to IP/device, and validate input against XSS.

Exam tip. Focus on "steal or predict the session token" as the mechanism, show the four-step flow, and list HTTPS and secure cookies as the principal defences.

Q17

Incident response & Bluetooth hacking

14 marks

(a) Describe the process of incident response with its steps. (b) Explain Bluetooth hacking and its types.

Answer

(a) Incident response process with its steps — 8 marks

Definition. Incident response (IR) is the organised, structured handling of a security incident — from first detection to full recovery and learning — carried out by a Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT). Its aim is to limit damage and restore normal operations with preserved evidence.

StepWhat happens
1. PreparationBuild the CSIRT, write policies and playbooks, train staff, deploy monitoring and ensure backups and tools are ready before an incident.
2. IdentificationDetect and verify the incident from alerts, logs or user reports; determine scope, affected assets and severity; begin documentation.
3. ContainmentStop the damage from spreading — isolate infected hosts, block IPs/accounts, disconnect the network — while preserving volatile evidence (RAM, process lists).
4. EradicationRemove the root cause: delete malware, remove unauthorised accounts, patch the exploited vulnerability and rebuild compromised systems from trusted media.
5. RecoveryRestore systems and data from clean backups, validate integrity, return services to production and monitor closely for re-infection.
6. Lessons learnedPost-incident review: what happened, what worked, what failed; update policies, controls and playbooks and archive the report.
  Prepare -> Identify -> Contain -> Eradicate -> Recover -> Lessons learned
     ^                                                        |
     +------------------- continuous improvement --------------+

Evidence handling. Follow order of volatility (CPU/RAM → network → disk → backups), image drives, hash evidence for integrity, keep a chain-of-custody log and avoid modifying originals. Example: on discovering ransomware, the team isolates the host (containment), identifies the entry phishing email, rebuilds the machine, restores files from offline backups and updates email filtering.

NIST SP 800-61 expresses the same cycle as: preparation; detection & analysis; containment, eradication & recovery; post-incident activity.

Exam tip. The six-step list is the whole answer — number every step, add "preserve evidence" and "chain of custody", and mention NIST SP 800-61 for full marks.

(b) Bluetooth hacking and its types — 6 marks

Definition. Bluetooth hacking targets the short-range wireless protocol (IEEE 802.15.1) used by phones, headsets, laptops and IoT devices. Attackers exploit weak pairing, discoverable modes, short PINs and unencrypted links. Tools include Bluesniff, BTcrack and Ubertooth.

Attack typeMechanism / effect

BluesnarfingExploits OBEX to read/copy contacts, messages, calendar and files without the owner's knowledge
BluebuggingTakes full control of a phone — makes calls, sends messages and eavesdrops via the microphone
BlueSmack / DoSFloods a device with oversized or repeated L2CAP packets, crashing or freezing it

Countermeasures: keep Bluetooth off when unused, stay non-discoverable, pair only in private, use long random PINs or modern Secure Simple Pairing with numeric comparison, apply firmware updates, and use encryption/authentication.

Q18

Reverse engineering & e-mail hacking

14 marks

(a) Describe the process of reverse engineering with its stages and tools. (b) Explain the different techniques used in e-mail hacking.

Answer

(a) Reverse engineering — stages and tools — 8 marks

Definition. Reverse engineering is the process of analysing a compiled program or malware to understand its design, logic and behaviour without the original source code. It is used for malware analysis, vulnerability research, interoperability and (illicitly) software cracking.

  Collect sample -> Static analysis -> Dynamic analysis -> Code/logic
   (hash, type)    (disassemble,        (run in sandbox,      understanding
                    strings, headers)    debug, observe)       -> Report
  1. Information gathering. Identify file type and architecture, compute MD5/SHA256 hashes, inspect headers/imports and extract readable strings (e.g. URLs, IPs, registry keys). Tools: file, hashsum, strings, PE/ELF explorers, VirusTotal.
  2. Static analysis. Examine the code without running it — disassemble with IDA Pro, Ghidra or objdump; decompile to pseudocode; map functions, imports and control flow.
  3. Dynamic analysis. Run the sample in an isolated sandbox/VM and observe behaviour: OllyDbg / x64dbg / GDB for debugging, Process Monitor, RegShot, Wireshark and sysmon for filesystem, registry and network activity.
  4. Code and logic analysis. Interpret the assembly/decompiled logic — identify encryption routines, command-and-control communication, persistence and payload triggers; possibly unpack or defeat anti-analysis.
  5. Documentation and reporting. Record indicators of compromise (IoCs), behaviour, capabilities and mitigation advice.
StageTools

DynamicOllyDbg, x64dbg, GDB, ProcMon, Wireshark, sandboxes (Cuckoo)

Example. A suspicious binary's strings reveal a URL; Ghidra shows a keylogging loop; running it in a sandbox confirms it beacons to a C2 server — producing IoCs for the IR team.

(b) Techniques used in e-mail hacking — 6 marks

E-mail attacks target the user, the message or the mail infrastructure:

  1. Phishing / spear phishing / whaling: deceptive emails lure users to fake login pages or make them disclose data; spear phishing targets a named individual, whaling targets executives.
  2. E-mail spoofing: forging the From header (SMTP does not authenticate senders by default) to impersonate a trusted contact or brand.
  3. Malicious attachments and links: infected documents/PDFs or drive-by links deliver malware or steal credentials.
  4. Business Email Compromise (BEC): impersonating an executive to authorise fraudulent fund transfers.
  5. Credential stuffing / brute force: trying leaked username–password pairs against webmail or SMTP accounts.
  6. Server-side attacks: exploiting an open mail relay to send spam, SMTP/POP3 vulnerabilities, or DNS/MX manipulation to redirect mail; header analysis (Received fields) is used to trace the true source.

Example. A fake "IT department" email with a spoofed From address asks staff to "verify your account" at a look-alike domain; one click harvests credentials.

Countermeasures: SPF, DKIM and DMARC, secure email gateways, attachment sandboxing, MFA, anti-phishing training, and disabling open relays.

Exam tip. Cover user-focused (phishing/spoofing) and server-focused (open relay, header analysis) techniques. Naming SPF/DKIM/DMARC as defences is a frequent bonus mark.

Module V — System hacking, hiding & log attacks

Q19

Covering tracks & event log categories

14 marks

(a) Illustrate how one can cover tracks and hide them. (b) Explain the six default categories used to classify event logs.

Answer

(a) Covering tracks and hiding them — 8 marks

Definition. Covering tracks (anti-forensics) is the set of techniques an attacker uses after an intrusion to erase, alter or hide evidence of their presence so investigators cannot reconstruct the attack and the attacker can maintain access.

  1. Clear or tamper with logs: delete security, system, web and application logs or individual entries — e.g. wevtutil cl Security on Windows, clearing /var/log on Linux, or disabling auditing with auditpol.
  2. Manipulate timestamps: "timestomp" to change a file's Modified/Accessed/Created (MAC) times so it blends in with legitimate files.
  3. Hide files and tools: use the hidden/system attributes, NTFS Alternate Data Streams, rootkits that intercept API calls, hidden partitions, slack space or steganography to conceal payloads.
  4. Disable or subvert monitoring: stop security services, kill EDR agents, clear browser history and shell history, and corrupt or redirect audit trails.
  5. Use stealthy persistence and execution: fileless malware living only in memory, registry run keys, scheduled tasks, process injection and living-off-the-land binaries (LOLbins) that leave few artefacts.
  6. Spoof identities and encrypt: create look-alike accounts, use stolen credentials and encrypt remaining data or communications to obstruct analysis.
   ATTACKER ACTION                ARTIFACT TARGET        DEFENCE
   --------------                ---------------        -------
   clear logs            ---->   Event logs      ---->  central SIEM
   timestomp             ---->   MAC times       ---->  file integrity
   hide in ADS/rootkit   ---->   file system     ---->  EDR/AV scan
   fileless in memory    ---->   RAM             ---->  memory forensics

Countermeasures: centralised, remote, append-only (WORM) logging with time synchronisation (NTP), file-integrity monitoring, EDR, strict privilege control and regular log review.

Exam tip. Group techniques as log clearing, timestamp manipulation, hidden storage, and stealthy execution, then give one defence each. The table plus the diagram satisfies "illustrate".

(b) Six default categories for classifying event logs — 6 marks

Windows event logging traditionally classifies records into six default log categories (the classic Windows Server set):

CategoryTypical contents
1. ApplicationEvents logged by applications and programs (errors, warnings, failures)
2. SecurityLogin/logout, account changes, access successes and failures, audit policy changes — the key forensic log
3. SystemOperating-system and driver events: service starts/stops, device errors, boot events
4. Directory ServiceActive Directory domain-controller events, replication and object changes
5. DNS ServerDNS queries, zone transfers and DNS service events

Forensic value. The Security log is the most important for intrusion investigation because it records authentication (Event ID 4624 success / 4625 failure), process creation (4688) and audit-policy changes (4719) — attackers target it first, which is why it must be forwarded off-host.

Exam tip. List the six categories in order with a one-line description each and add that Security is the key log for investigators. If short on time, give the five modern Windows Logs as a backup line.
Q20

Password sniffing & log-file defences

14 marks

(a) With a diagram, explain the password sniffing method. (b) What are the defences against logs and accounting file attacks?

Answer

(a) Password sniffing method with diagram — 8 marks

Definition. Password sniffing is the capture of user credentials as they travel across a network in cleartext or weakly protected form. A sniffer in promiscuous mode records packets, and a protocol analyser extracts the username/password fields from protocols such as HTTP Basic, FTP, Telnet, POP3, IMAP, SNMP v1 and VNC.

                       (1) normal login: user/pass in cleartext
   [Victim PC] ----------------------> [Server]
          \                              /
           \  (2) attacker captures via /
            \     hub / promiscuous NIC /
             v                        v
            [ ATTACKER: Wireshark / tcpdump ]
             reads USER=alice  PASS=secret123
                       |
        (or ARP/DHCP spoofing first to force
         traffic through the attacker)
  1. Gain a vantage point: connect to a hub or open Wi-Fi network, put the NIC in promiscuous mode, or use ARP/DHCP spoofing to make traffic flow through the attacker on a switched LAN.
  2. Capture traffic: run a sniffer (Wireshark, tcpdump, Ettercap) and filter for authentication ports — FTP 21, Telnet 23, HTTP 80, POP3 110, IMAP 143, SNMP 161.
  3. Extract credentials: follow the TCP stream and read the USER/PASS strings; for a challenge-response protocol, capture the hashes and crack them offline.
  4. Replay / use: log in with the recovered credentials, then cover tracks by clearing logs.

Example. On an open café Wi-Fi, an attacker captures an FTP session and reads USER admin / PASS summer2024 directly from the packet bytes.

Countermeasures: never use cleartext protocols — use SSH/SFTP/SCP instead of Telnet/FTP, HTTPS instead of HTTP, IMAPS/POP3S, SNMPv3; enforce WPA2/3-Enterprise, VPNs, switched networks with port security and Dynamic ARP Inspection, MFA and strong unique passwords.

Exam tip. The diagram must show victim, attacker-with-sniffer and server, label "promiscuous mode / cleartext" and include the ARP-spoof alternative. Finish with "encrypt the protocol" as the primary defence.

(b) Defences against logs and accounting file attacks — 6 marks

Because attackers delete or alter logs to hide their tracks, defences aim to make log records complete, tamper-evident and independent of the target host:

  1. Centralised remote logging: forward logs immediately to a separate SIEM/syslog server so an attacker who owns the host cannot erase the copies held elsewhere.
  2. Append-only / WORM storage and backups: write-once media and offline, immutable backups prevent alteration or deletion of archived records.
  3. File-integrity monitoring: hash critical logs and config files and alert on any change (Tripwire, AIDE, OSSEC).
  4. Access control and least privilege: restrict who can read, write or clear logs; remove unnecessary log-clearing rights; protect audit policies from being disabled.
  5. Time synchronisation: NTP/PTP keeps timestamps consistent across hosts so events can be correlated reliably.
  6. Monitoring, alerting and correlation: alert on log-clearing events (e.g. Event ID 1102), gaps in sequence numbers, or disabled auditing; use a SIEM to correlate events and detect tampering in real time.

Example. An attacker clears the local Security log, but the SIEM had already collected each event; the gap and the 1102 "log cleared" alert expose the intrusion.

Exam tip. The single most important idea is "ship logs off the host to a central, immutable store". Add hashing, NTP and alerting on log-clear events for a full six marks.

Q2

Vulnerability Assessment in Penetration Testing

3 marks

What is vulnerability assessment in penetration testing?

Answer
Vulnerability assessment (VA) is the systematic process of discovering, classifying and prioritising security weaknesses in a system, network or application. Unlike a full penetration test, it identifies and reports flaws but does not necessarily exploit them.

Mechanism / procedure:

  1. Define scope — which hosts, networks or applications are in scope and what tooling is allowed.
  2. Discover assets — enumerate live hosts, open ports and running services (for example with a port scanner).
  3. Scan — automated scanners (Nessus, OpenVAS, Qualys) probe for known CVEs, misconfigurations and weak defaults.
  4. Analyse and validate — remove false positives and assign severity (CVSS score: Low / Medium / High / Critical).
  5. Report and remediate — rank by risk and recommend patches or configuration changes.

Example: A scan reveals an Apache server missing a security patch (a High CVE) and an FTP service allowing anonymous login (Medium). The report recommends patching and disabling anonymous access.

Note: VA is breadth-first and largely automated; penetration testing is depth-first and proves exploitability by chaining flaws. A VA answers "what is wrong?"; a pen test answers "can it actually be broken, and how far can an attacker get?"
Exam tip: Contrast VA with penetration testing in one sentence — VA = automated discovery + prioritisation; PT = manual exploitation + impact demonstration. Include the CVSS/CVE terms; they are high-value keywords.

Further exam angle

Exam tip. A 3-marker wants the sequence, not essays. Write the seven phases as a numbered list and keep "authorisation" and "reporting" in — examiners frequently award a mark for each of those bookends.

Definition. Penetration testing (pen testing) is an authorised, simulated attack on a computer system, network or application, performed to discover security weaknesses before a real attacker does. Unlike a vulnerability scan, it actively exploits the weaknesses found.

Social Engineering and Example

3 marks

Define social engineering and give an example.

Answer
Social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information. Instead of attacking software, the attacker exploits human traits such as trust, fear, curiosity, urgency, helpfulness and authority.

Mechanism: The attacker researches the target, adopts a credible pretext (a role or story), establishes trust or creates time pressure, extracts information or gains a physical/logical access, and then uses the obtained data for further attack. Common channels are phone (vishing), email (phishing), SMS (smishing), in person and social media.

Impact: Credential theft, unauthorised access, financial fraud and malware installation. Countermeasures: security-awareness training, verification procedures, least privilege and an obligation to report suspicious contact.

Q4

Shoulder Surfing

3 marks

What is shoulder surfing in the context of social engineering attacks?

Answer
Shoulder surfing is a direct-observation social engineering technique in which an attacker secretly watches a victim to obtain sensitive information such as a PIN, password, OTP or confidential screen content.

Mechanism: The attacker positions themselves where they can see the victim's screen or keyboard — over a shoulder in a queue, across a train aisle, at an ATM, or through a window/reflection. They observe keystrokes, screen display, or even the pattern of a touchscreen, and later replay what they learned. It can be aided by binoculars, a phone camera or a small mirror; a related high-tech variant uses shoulder-surfing malware or screen-recording trojans.

Example: At an ATM, a person standing immediately behind a customer watches the keypad to record the 4-digit PIN, then later steals the card (or skims it) and withdraws cash. In an office, a visitor reading a colleague's monitor can note an admin password typed during a demonstration.

Countermeasures: Privacy screens/filters, careful positioning, shielding the keypad while typing, randomising keypads, biometric authentication, awareness training and challenging strangers in secure areas.

Exam tip: Classify shoulder surfing as a physical/direct observation technique (no conversation, unlike phishing or pretexting). A one-line definition plus the ATM example plus two defences is a complete 3-mark response.

Q5

ARP Spoofing

3 marks

What is ARP spoofing, and how does it work?

Answer
ARP spoofing (ARP poisoning) is an attack in which an adversary sends forged Address Resolution Protocol messages over a local area network to associate their own MAC address with the IP address of another host — typically the default gateway.

Mechanism: ARP maps a known IPv4 address to an unknown MAC address on a LAN and is stateless and unauthenticated — any host may announce an IP-to-MAC binding, and machines cache the answer. The attacker floods the victim's ARP cache with replies claiming that the gateway's IP maps to the attacker's MAC, and possibly claims to the gateway that the victim's IP maps to the attacker's MAC. The victim's cache is "poisoned". Subsequent frames destined for the gateway are sent to the attacker's NIC.

Result: The attacker can silently relay traffic (enabling a Man-in-the-Middle attack), sniff credentials, or simply drop the traffic to cause a denial of service. Because ARP only works inside a broadcast domain, the attack is limited to the local network segment.

Victim → intends to send to Gateway IP 192.168.1.1 Attacker advertises: "192.168.1.1 is at MAC AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF (attacker)" Victim's ARP cache now maps gateway IP → attacker MAC → traffic diverted.

Countermeasures: Static ARP entries for critical hosts, DHCP snooping, Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI) on switches, port security, encryption (HTTPS/SSH/VPN), and ARP-monitoring tools.

Exam tip: The root cause to state explicitly is "ARP has no authentication". Mention that it works only on the local broadcast domain and that the payload is a forged ARP reply.

Further exam angle

Pitfall: State clearly that ARP spoofing causes DoS when packets are dropped, and becomes MitM when packets are forwarded — examiners reward this distinction.
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) give four weak-auth types, a mechanism chain, one example and the fix list. For (b) draw the ARP cache before/after diagram, number the DoS steps, separate DoS from MitM, and give four countermeasures naming DAI + DHCP snooping and port security explicitly.

Man-in-the-Middle Attacks

3 marks

Define Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attacks.

Answer
A Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attack occurs when an attacker secretly inserts themselves into a communication channel between two parties who believe they are talking directly to each other. The attacker can listen to, capture, alter or inject the traffic, while both endpoints remain unaware.

Mechanism: The attacker must first position themselves on the path and then intercept the session. Common ways to achieve this position are ARP spoofing on a LAN, DNS spoofing/poisoning, rogue Wi-Fi access points (Evil Twin), HTTPS spoofing or malicious proxies. Once in the middle, the attacker relays messages between the victims to avoid suspicion while reading or modifying them — for example, capturing session cookies or changing the recipient's bank account number in a transaction.

Alice ⇄ [ ATTACKER ] ⇄ Bob Alice believes she talks to Bob; Bob believes he talks to Alice. Attacker can: eavesdrop ↓ modify ✎ inject + replay ↻

Example: On a public Wi-Fi network, an attacker sets up a rogue access point named like the cafe's real one. Victims connect, and all their HTTP traffic passes through the attacker, who steals login cookies.

Countermeasures: End-to-end encryption (TLS/HTTPS with HSTS), certificate pinning, mutual authentication, VPNs, disabling auto-connect to open Wi-Fi, and network IDS detection of ARP/DNS anomalies.

Exam tip: Distinguish passive MITM (only eavesdropping) from active MITM (modifying/injecting). Always pair the definition with one way the attacker gets into the middle (ARP/DNS/rogue AP) — that earns the mechanism mark.

Q7

SQL Injection

3 marks

What is SQL injection, and how does it affect web applications?

Answer
SQL injection (SQLi) is a web application vulnerability in which untrusted user input is concatenated into a SQL query and interpreted as code rather than as data. It arises when applications fail to validate or properly parameterise input.

Mechanism: The attacker submits input containing SQL syntax (for example a quote followed by an always-true condition such as ' OR '1'='1) into a form field or URL parameter. Because the input is built directly into the query string, the database executes the altered statement. By varying the input, the attacker can bypass authentication, read tables they should not see (UNION-based), infer data through true/false responses (blind SQLi), or, with sufficient privileges, modify or delete records.

Effect on web applications: Unauthorised access to user accounts, disclosure of entire databases (credentials, card data), data corruption or deletion, and in some configurations escalation to operating-system command execution and full server compromise. It also causes legal and reputational damage.

Countermeasures: Parameterised queries / prepared statements (the primary defence), stored procedures, input validation and allow-listing, least-privilege database accounts, ORM usage, disabling verbose error messages, and a WAF as defence in depth.

Pitfall: Escaping special characters alone is not a reliable fix. The single strongest answer is "use parameterised queries so data is never treated as code".
Exam tip: State the root cause (untrusted input concatenated into a query), one attack idea (authentication bypass or data theft), and the main fix (prepared statements). Keep any example conceptual — do not need to write a working payload.

Q8

Session Hijacking

3 marks

What is session hijacking, and how does it threaten web applications?

Answer
Session hijacking is an attack in which an adversary takes over a legitimate user's active session by stealing or predicting the session identifier (session ID / token), thereby gaining the same access as the authenticated user without knowing the password.

Mechanism: Web applications are stateless, so they track logged-in users with a session token stored in a cookie or URL. If an attacker obtains that token — by sniffing unencrypted traffic, cross-site scripting (XSS), a MITM/ARP-poisoning attack, predicting a weak token, or fixing a session ID — the attacker replays it in their own requests. The server sees a valid token and treats the attacker as the victim. Session fixation is the related variant where the attacker forces a known session ID on the victim first.

Threat: Account takeover, fraudulent transactions, access to personal or administrative functions, and bypass of two-factor authentication for the duration of the session. Because the attacker uses a genuine session, the activity can look legitimate in logs.

Countermeasures: Enforce HTTPS/HSTS with Secure and HttpOnly cookie flags, regenerate the session ID after login, use long random tokens, implement idle/absolute timeouts, bind sessions to IP/device heuristics, and log out on suspicious activity.

Exam tip: The key phrase is "stealing or predicting the session token, not the password". Link it to MITM and XSS as the usual delivery methods; that ties several syllabus topics together and earns full marks.

Q9

Event Logging

3 marks

What is event logging in the context of cybersecurity?

Answer
Event logging is the automatic recording of significant activities and events — login attempts, file access, process starts, configuration changes, errors and network connections — into a chronological log, so that the state and behaviour of a system can be reconstructed later.

Mechanism: Operating systems, applications, firewalls and IDSs generate log entries containing a timestamp, source, event ID, severity and relevant detail (user, IP, object). On Windows these appear in the Event Viewer (Security, System, Application logs); on Linux in syslog//var/log. Logs may be local or forwarded to a central SIEM for correlation. They support detection (spotting brute force or unusual traffic), incident investigation and forensics (timeline reconstruction), compliance/audit evidence, and troubleshooting.

Example: A series of Event ID 4625 "failed logon" entries from one IP, followed by a 4624 success, reveals a brute-force attack that finally guessed a password.

Defensive considerations: Protect logs from tampering by attackers (write-once storage, central collection, access control), synchronise time with NTP, retain them long enough, and monitor alerts. Attackers, conversely, try to clear or forge logs to cover their tracks.

Exam tip: Give the definition, then name the four purposes — detection, forensics, compliance, troubleshooting — and finish with the observation that logs themselves are a target for attackers (log wiping). That shows both Blue-team and Red-team awareness.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Name the goal (anti-forensics / destroying the audit trail), list one or two methods (delete, edit, timestamp change), and mention the key defence — centralised, write-once, time-synced log servers — for a complete 3-mark response.

Definition. Hiding evidence by altering event logs — often called log tampering, log scrubbing or anti-forensics — is the process of modifying, deleting or corrupting audit records so that an intruder's actions cannot be reconstructed. It is an anti-forensic technique aimed at defeating accountability and incident investigation.

Mechanism. After gaining access, an attacker edits or truncates log files (/var/log/*, Windows Event Logs), deletes specific event IDs, changes timestamps, or clears the entire log with utilities such as wevtutil cl. More subtle attacks remove only the attacker's own entries to avoid the suspicion a completely empty log would cause. Distributed or forwarded logs may be intercepted before they reach the collector.

Q10

Password Cracking

3 marks

Explain the concept of password cracking.

Answer
Password cracking is the process of recovering a password from stored or transmitted data — usually a hash — without initially knowing it. It is the automated testing of candidate passwords against a target until the correct one is found.

Mechanism: Systems rarely store plaintext passwords; they store a one-way hash. An attacker who obtains the hash file (from a breach, a compromised host, or a captured authenticator) generates candidate passwords, hashes each one with the same algorithm and compares the result to the stored hash. A match reveals the plaintext. Methods include: dictionary attacks (word lists), brute force (all character combinations), hybrid attacks (words plus number/symbol substitutions), rule-based attacks, rainbow tables (precomputed hashes), and credential stuffing (reusing passwords leaked from other sites). GPUs and wordlist rules make this fast.

Example: An NTLM hash is captured; a dictionary attack tries common passwords and finds "Summer2024!", granting access to the account.

Why it matters / mitigation: Weak or reused passwords fall quickly. Defences: salted, slow password-hashing algorithms (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2), strong password policy, multi-factor authentication, account lockout/rate limiting, and limiting hash exposure. Ethically it is used by auditors to test password strength and in forensics with authorisation.

Exam tip: Emphasise that cracking usually works on hashes, not plaintext, and that salting defeats rainbow tables while slow KDFs defeat brute force. One method + one mitigation completes the answer.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Tie non-repudiation to the CIA triad's often forgotten companion services — authentication, integrity and audit/logs. Name digital signatures + PKI + timestamps as the enabling mechanisms and contrast with MACs for a distinguished answer.

Definition. Non-repudiation is the security service that prevents a party from denying that it performed an action — such as sending a message, signing a document or approving a transaction. It provides proof of origin and proof of delivery so the sender cannot claim "I never sent that" and the receiver cannot claim "I never received it."

Q14

DoS Attacks & Trojans vs Backdoors

14 marks

Module II — Q14: (a) Explain the concept of denial-of-service (DoS) attacks. (b) What are the differences between Trojans and backdoors, and how do they affect network security?

Answer
Part (a) — Denial-of-Service attacks (6 marks)

A Denial-of-Service (DoS) attack intentionally overwhelms a system, service or network so that legitimate users cannot access it — an attack on availability, one arm of the CIA triad. In a distributed DoS (DDoS), the flood originates from many compromised machines (a botnet), making it far harder to stop.

Categories:

  1. Volumetric / flooding — saturate bandwidth with traffic (UDP/ICMP floods, amplification via DNS/NTP/SSDP).
  2. Protocol attacks — exhaust connection state or resources (SYN flood, Ping of Death, fragmented packets).
  3. Application-layer attacks — target expensive functions (HTTP GET floods, slowloris) with few packets but high cost.

Q15

Remote Exploitation & MITM with Session Hijacking

14 marks

Module III — Q15: (a) Describe how remote exploitation works in the context of network attacks. (b) Explain the steps involved in conducting an MITM attack, including session hijacking.

Answer
Part (a) — How remote exploitation works (6 marks)

Remote exploitation is the act of compromising a machine or application over a network without prior physical or local access, by triggering a vulnerability in a service that listens on a port. It is the core of most network attacks because it turns information gathered during scanning into actual control.

Mechanism / steps:

  1. Discover a reachable host and enumerate open ports/services and versions (e.g., an unpatched web server or SMB service).
  2. Identify a vulnerability in that service — a known CVE, missing patch, weak configuration or logic flaw.
  3. Select or craft an exploit that reliably triggers the flaw (a payload delivered over the network to the listening port).
  4. Deliver the payload to obtain a shell or code execution on the target — this is Remote Code Execution (RCE).
  5. Post-exploitation: escalate privileges, dump credentials, pivot to other hosts and establish persistence.

Example: An unpatched, internet-facing service with a known buffer-overflow CVE is exploited, giving the attacker a remote shell as a low-privileged user. Countermeasures: prompt patching, hardening/disabling unused services, least privilege, network segmentation, IDS/IPS, WAF and exposure reduction (firewalls). Remote exploitation differs from local exploitation in that the attacker needs no account on the machine first.

Part (b) — Steps in an MITM attack including session hijacking (8 marks)

A Man-in-the-Middle attack places the attacker in the traffic path. When the goal is account takeover, it commonly leads to session hijacking — stealing the victim's session token and replaying it.

Victim ──(1 poison ARP)──▶ Attacker ──(relay)──▶ Gateway Victim's browser ──(2 plaintext HTTP cookie)──▶ passes through Attacker Attacker ──(3 replay cookie)──▶ Web server: "It's me, the victim!" → logged in

Steps:

  1. Position: poison the victim's ARP cache (or DNS, or run a rogue Wi-Fi AP / Evil Twin) so traffic flows through the attacker.
  2. Enable forwarding: transparently relay packets so the connection continues and neither side notices.
  3. Capture: sniff traffic for credentials and, critically, the session cookie — easy when the site uses plain HTTP; HTTPS requires certificate tricks/downgrade.
  4. Hijack: inject the stolen cookie into the attacker's own browser (cookie/session replay) to assume the authenticated identity; optionally use session fixation (force a known session ID first).
  5. Act & cover: perform fraudulent actions and hide traces in logs.

Countermeasures: HTTPS everywhere with HSTS, Secure/HttpOnly/SameSite cookies, certificate pinning, VPN on untrusted Wi-Fi, dynamic ARP inspection, IDS alerts for ARP/DNS anomalies, session-ID regeneration after login and short timeouts.

Exam tip: For (a), use the chain discover → find flaw → craft/deliver payload → shell → escalate; mention RCE explicitly. For (b), tie ARP spoofing to MITM to cookie capture to replay — examiners love seeing the topics linked. End both parts with defences.
Q16

Brute Force & ARP Spoofing to DoS

14 marks

Module III — Q16: (a) What are brute force attacks, and how do they compromise system security? (b) How does ARP spoofing lead to denial-of-service attacks, and what are its countermeasures?

Answer
Part (a) — Brute-force attacks (6 marks)

A brute-force attack tries every possible (or a large set of) credential combinations until the correct one works. It is exhaustive trial-and-error, automated by tools that test thousands of guesses per second, often accelerated by GPUs and distributed botnets.

Variants: pure brute force (all character combinations), dictionary attack (common words + rules), hybrid (words with appended digits/symbols), credential stuffing (reusing leaked username/password pairs), and password spraying (a few common passwords across many accounts to avoid lockouts). Against hashes offline, the attacker compares computed hashes to a stolen hash file; online, guesses are submitted to a login form.

How it compromises security: Once a password is guessed, the attacker gains unauthorised access, escalates privileges, exfiltrates data or pivots deeper. Weak, short or reused passwords fall quickly — the effective search space is small — and the attack is noisy but often detectable in logs as many failed logins.

Countermeasures: account lockout and exponential backoff/rate limiting, CAPTCHA, strong password policy and passphrases, salted slow hashing (bcrypt/Argon2) for stored passwords, MFA (which defeats a guessed password alone), monitoring/alerting on failed-login bursts, and breached-password checks.

Part (b) — ARP spoofing leading to DoS, and countermeasures (8 marks)

ARP spoofing poisons a host's IP-to-MAC cache with forged replies. Beyond enabling eavesdropping/MITM, it can directly cause a Denial of Service by making traffic disappear or be corrupted.

Normal: Victim → gateway's REAL MAC → Internet ✓ Poisoned: Victim → attacker's MAC (claimed as gateway) Case 1: attacker drops packets → victim offline (DoS) Case 2: attacker forwards to a wrong/dead MAC → no reply (DoS) Case 3: attacker floods forged ARP → cache thrashing, LAN instability Case 4: attacker impersonates the victim to the gateway → victim's traffic black-holed

Mechanism / steps:

  1. Attacker sends forged gratuitous ARP replies claiming the gateway IP maps to a non-existent or attacker MAC.
  2. Victim and/or switch caches the false binding; frames are sent to the wrong layer-2 destination.
  3. Because the real gateway never receives them, sessions time out and services become unreachable — availability is lost.
  4. Mass poisoning (ARP storm) can overwhelm switches and trigger network-wide outages.

Countermeasures: static ARP entries on critical hosts; switch features — DHCP snooping, Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), port security and VLAN segmentation; ARP-cache monitoring tools; encryption (HTTPS/VPN) so misdirected traffic is useless; and redundancy/IDS alerting. Note that encryption protects confidentiality but not availability — DAI/static ARP are the real fixes for the DoS.

Exam tip: For (b), make the causal chain explicit: forged ARP → wrong MAC → traffic misdirected/dropped → service unavailable. Then separate defences into host-level (static ARP), switch-level (DAI/DHCP snooping) and data-level (encryption), noting encryption alone does not stop the DoS — a nuance that impresses examiners.

Routers, Firewalls & Reverse Engineering

14 marks

Module IV — Q17: (a) Discuss the role of routers and firewalls in protecting a network. (b) Explain the process of reverse engineering and how it is used in identifying vulnerabilities.

Answer
Part (a) — Role of routers and firewalls (6 marks)

Routers operate at OSI Layer 3, forwarding packets between networks using IP addresses and routing tables. Their security role comes from Access Control Lists (ACLs) that permit or deny traffic by source/destination IP, protocol and port, plus NAT (hiding internal addressing), traffic segmentation through VLANs/subnets, rate limiting and logging. They shape what can reach a network at all.

Firewalls enforce a security policy at the network boundary — allowing trusted traffic and blocking untrusted traffic. Types: packet-filtering (stateless, ACL-based), stateful inspection (tracks connection state), proxy/application-level and Web Application Firewalls (inspect HTTP), and next-generation firewalls (deep packet inspection, IPS, app awareness). Firewalls implement default-deny, DMZ placement and egress filtering.

Internet
    |
 [Router]  - ACLs, NAT, rate-limiting
    |
 [Firewall]- stateful rules, DMZ, IDS/IPS
    |
  +-+-----------------+---------------+
  | DMZ: web/mail/DNS | Internal LAN  |
  +-------------------+---------------+

Combined protection: the router filters at the network edge and routes only allowed traffic; the firewall inspects and enforces policy statefully, isolating the DMZ from internal resources. Together they reduce the attack surface, contain lateral movement and generate logs for detection. Limitations: they cannot stop insiders, encrypted malicious traffic or application flaws behind allowed ports — hence defence in depth with IDS and hardening.

Part (b) — Reverse engineering and its role in finding vulnerabilities (8 marks)

Reverse engineering is the systematic analysis of a compiled program, binary or malware to understand its structure, behaviour and logic without the original source code. It deconstructs the artefact to reveal what it actually does.

Process:

  1. Static analysis — inspect the binary without running it: file type/strings, imports, headers, disassemblers (IDA Pro, Ghidra, radare2) and decompilers to recover pseudo-code and control flow.
  2. Dynamic analysis — run it in a safe sandbox/debugger (OllyDbg, x64dbg, GDB), tracing execution, registers, memory, API calls and network behaviour.
  3. Interpret and document — reconstruct algorithms, locate interesting functions (parsers, crypto, authentication) and map inputs to code paths.

How it identifies vulnerabilities: Analysts examine input-handling routines for unsafe calls (e.g., strcpy, unbounded copies) that indicate buffer overflows, inspect crypto for weak keys, check authorization logic for flaws, and use patch diffing — comparing a patched binary with the vulnerable one — to pinpoint the exact flaw a vendor fixed and derive a signature. Combined with fuzzing of the recovered input paths, reverse engineering exposes zero-day vulnerabilities, confirms exploitability of CVEs, and supports malware analysis (understanding C2, persistence, indicators of compromise). It also verifies that a fix is complete.

Ethical/legal note: Reverse engineering must be done on systems you own or are authorised to analyse; it is legitimate for malware research, interoperability and defensive patch analysis, but unauthorised analysis may breach licences/laws.

Exam tip: For (a), contrast the router's Layer-3 ACL/NAT role with the firewall's stateful policy enforcement, and draw the simple perimeter diagram. For (b), give static + dynamic analysis, name at least two tools, and explain patch diffing and fuzzing as the bridge from "understanding code" to "finding a vulnerability".
Q18

Buffer Overflow & Writing an Exploit

14 marks

Module IV — Q18: (a) What is buffer overflow, and what are its implications in web security? (b) Describe the steps involved in writing an exploit and its potential risks.

Answer
Part (a) — Buffer overflow and its web-security implications (6 marks)

A buffer overflow occurs when a program writes more data into a fixed-size memory buffer than it can hold, so the excess overwrites adjacent memory. It is caused by missing bounds checks in languages such as C/C++ (unsafe functions like strcpy, gets). In a stack overflow, the overflow can overwrite the saved return address (EIP/RIP), letting an attacker redirect execution to injected code; a heap overflow corrupts heap metadata or function pointers.

Stack grows down:
  [ local buffer 256 bytes ]  <-- user input (too long)
  [ saved EBP               ]
  [ return address (EIP)    ]  <-- overwritten -> jump to attacker's code
  [ function arguments      ]

Implications in web security: Many web servers, CGI binaries, native modules and embedded devices are written in C/C++; an overflow in an internet-facing component can give remote code execution, crashes/DoS, privilege escalation or data leakage. Classic web-related cases include overflows in server extensions, image/parser libraries and protocol handlers. Even where web apps use managed languages, the underlying stack (web server, interpreter) can be vulnerable.

Countermeasures: safe libraries and bounds-checked functions, input-length validation, ASLR (randomises addresses), DEP/NX (marks stack non-executable), stack canaries, compiler fortification, memory-safe languages (Rust, Go, Java), fuzzing and patching.

Part (b) — Steps in developing an exploit (conceptual) and its risks (8 marks)

An exploit is a program or script that takes advantage of a specific vulnerability to produce a desired effect (a shell, a crash, privilege escalation). Described conceptually, the development workflow is:

  1. Find the flaw — identify a vulnerable application/version and a reproducible crash or behaviour (via fuzzing, CVE research or code review).
  2. Control the instruction pointer — determine the exact input offset that overwrites the saved return address so execution can be redirected.
  3. Prepare a payload — choose a payload (shellcode / ROP chain) appropriate to the goal, avoiding "bad characters" that break it.
  4. Locate a reliable jump target — account for ASLR/DEP (e.g., ROP gadgets, jmp/call registers), and build the final input combining filler, redirect address and payload.
  5. Test and refine — run against a lab copy, handle variations, then package it as a reusable exploit module.
  6. Deliver — send it to the target service through the vulnerable input channel.

Potential risks: Exploits are fragile — a different OS, patch level, compiler or offset can crash the service (DoS) or simply fail; failed attempts are logged and may trigger intrusion detection; shellcode may be blocked by AV/EDR; and most importantly, using an exploit against systems without authorisation is illegal and can cause real damage. Ethical use is limited to authorised labs and engagements with rules of engagement.

Exam tip: For (a), include the stack layout diagram and pair each mechanism with its modern defence (canary ↔ overwrite, ASLR ↔ address, NX ↔ execution). For (b), present the numbered workflow but keep it conceptual — no real shellcode — and stress fragility, detection and legality as the "risks".

Password cracking is central to system hacking because access credentials are the gateway to the whole system. Its importance:

  1. Initial access — recover passwords from captured hashes to log in as a legitimate user.

  • Enable MFA so a cracked password alone is insufficient;
  • Account lockout / rate limiting, CAPTCHA and alerting on failed logins;
  • Protect hash stores (least privilege, LAPS for unique local admin passwords), and patch to limit hash theft (e.g., credential guard).

  2. Timestomp — change file MAC times (created/modified/accessed) so malicious files appear old or blend with system files.

Memory forensics can recover evidence that disk cleanup misses.

Q2

Non-repudiation

3 marks

Briefly explain the concept of non-repudiation in information security.

Answer

Definition. Non-repudiation is the security service that prevents a party from denying that it performed an action — such as sending a message, signing a document or approving a transaction. It provides proof of origin and proof of delivery so the sender cannot claim "I never sent that" and the receiver cannot claim "I never received it."

Mechanism. Non-repudiation is built on digital signatures and public-key infrastructure (PKI). The sender hashes the message, encrypts the hash with their private key to create a signature, and sends message + signature. Anyone can verify with the sender's public key; because only the owner holds the private key, the signature is strong evidence of origin. Hash functions give integrity (the message was not altered) and timestamps/audit logs add temporal proof. Message Authentication Codes give integrity and authentication but not true non-repudiation, because both parties share the key.

Example. An online purchase is signed with the customer's digital certificate and time-stamped by a trusted authority; the bank cannot later repudiate the order, and the customer cannot deny placing it.

Exam tip: Tie non-repudiation to the CIA triad's often forgotten companion services — authentication, integrity and audit/logs. Name digital signatures + PKI + timestamps as the enabling mechanisms and contrast with MACs for a distinguished answer.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: Begin with the CIA triad, then add authenticity, non-repudiation and accountability for full marks. One crisp definition plus one concrete mechanism per element is the expected 3-mark pattern.

Answer

What is network sniffing, and what are its types?

Answer

Definition. Network sniffing (packet capture) is the act of intercepting and inspecting raw data packets travelling across a network using a packet analyser such as Wireshark or tcpdump. It turns the shared network medium into a place to observe traffic that was not addressed to the sniffer.

Types. (1) Passive sniffing — on a hub or via a SPAN/mirror port, the sniffer silently receives copies of traffic without altering it. Example. On an open Wi-Fi network an attacker captures HTTP login traffic and reads plaintext credentials; on a switched LAN they first ARP-poison the gateway to route traffic through their machine.

Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)

3 marks

What is cross-site scripting (XSS)?

Answer

Definition. Cross-site scripting (XSS) is a client-side injection vulnerability in which an attacker injects malicious JavaScript into a web page that is then executed in the victim's browser, running in the site's origin with the victim's privileges.

Mechanism. A web application echoes user input into a page without validating or escaping it. Main types. Stored (persistent) — payload saved in the database and served to many victims; Reflected — payload in the request is bounced back in the response (phishing links); DOM-based — the flaw is in client-side JavaScript manipulating the DOM.

IDS vs IPS

3 marks

What is the difference between IDS and IPS in network security?

Answer

Definition. An IDS (Intrusion Detection System) is a passive monitoring device that detects and alerts on suspicious activity; an IPS (Intrusion Prevention System) is an inline device that detects and actively blocks the malicious traffic in real time.

Mechanism and differences. An IDS sits out-of-band, receiving a copy of traffic via a SPAN port or tap — it never sits in the packet path, so it cannot stop an attack, only alert, log or trigger a response. An IPS is deployed inline so all traffic passes through it; on detecting a signature or anomaly it drops the packet, resets the connection or blocks the source. Consequently an IPS can cause false-positive outages and adds latency, while an IDS is safer to deploy but reactive.

Q10

Privilege Escalation

3 marks

What is privilege escalation?

Answer

Definition. Privilege escalation is an attack in which a user or process gains access rights or permissions higher than those legitimately granted — for example moving from a normal user to administrator or root.

Mechanism. Vertical escalation raises privilege within one account (user to admin) by exploiting a kernel/OS bug, a vulnerable setuid binary, misconfigured sudo rules, stored credentials or an unpatched service running as root. Horizontal escalation moves sideways to another account with similar privileges (stealing a peer's session or credentials). Attackers first enumerate the OS, users, running processes and installed patches, then match a weakness to a known exploit.

Example. A compromised low-privilege account runs a local kernel exploit that adds a new root user, converting a foothold into full system control; from there the attacker can disable defences and reach the whole network.

Exam tip: Always define vertical vs horizontal, give one vector (unpatched kernel / misconfigured sudo / stolen token) and one countermeasure (least privilege, patching, UAC, auditing). This reliably earns all 3 marks.

Further exam angle

Exam tip: The decisive phrase is "same privilege level, different user". Always contrast it in one line with vertical escalation to prove you know the difference.

Horizontal privilege escalation (horizontal movement) is an attack in which a user accesses the data or functions of another user who has the same privilege level, without elevating their own rights. It contrasts with vertical escalation, where a low-privilege user becomes an administrator or root.

Mechanism: the attacker first compromises one account — by password guessing, phishing, session-token theft or Pass-the-Hash — and then uses that legitimate identity to reach peers' resources: another user's mailbox, home directory or application records. On networks, "lateral movement" uses the same idea with tools such as PsExec, WMI or stolen Kerberos tickets to hop from one workstation to the next.

(a) Role of security and penetration testers (6 marks)

Definitions. A security tester evaluates the overall security posture of an organisation — policies, controls, configurations and awareness — to find weaknesses before attackers do. (b) How non-repudiation enhances security in online communication (8 marks)

Definition. Non-repudiation is the assurance that an entity cannot deny the authenticity of its actions — that a specific party sent, received or authorised a specific message. In online communication it supplies irrefutable evidence, backed by cryptography and trusted third parties, that a transaction really occurred.

Mechanism — how it is achieved.

  1. The sender hashes the message to create a fixed-size digest.
  2. The digest is encrypted with the sender's private key to form a digital signature (RSA/ECDSA) — only the owner can produce it.
  3. The receiver verifies with the sender's public key obtained from a digital certificate issued by a trusted Certificate Authority in a PKI, confirming identity and integrity.
  4. A trusted Time-Stamping Authority and secure audit logs record when the event happened, preventing back-dating or later denial.
 Sender                                      Receiver
   |  hash(message)                              |
   |  sign(hash) with PRIVATE key                |
   | ------ message + signature + cert --------> |
   |                                 verify with PUBLIC key (from CA cert)
   |                                 compare hash -> integrity + origin + time
   | <------------------ signed receipt --------- |  (proof of delivery)

Enhancements to online communication security.

  • Accountability — every action is attributable to a named key holder, discouraging fraud and misuse.
  • Integrity and authenticity — the recipient knows the message is unaltered and genuinely from the claimed sender.
  • Dispute resolution / legal proof — signed, time-stamped records satisfy regulators and courts (e.g. e-contracts, e-tendering, banking). Essential for e-commerce, e-governance and online banking.
  • Defence against replay and forgery — timestamps and nonces stop an attacker reusing a captured message.

Contrast. Symmetric MACs (HMAC) give integrity and authentication but not non-repudiation because both parties hold the shared key; only asymmetric digital signatures provide true non-repudiation.

Pitfall: Non-repudiation is not the same as confidentiality — signatures prove who sent something but do not hide it; combine with encryption for both.

(a) DNS spoofing and its consequences (6 marks)

Definition. DNS spoofing (DNS poisoning/cache poisoning) is an attack in which false DNS mapping data is introduced into a resolver's cache so that a legitimate hostname resolves to an attacker-controlled IP address. Victims who type the correct URL are silently redirected to a malicious site.

Mechanism. The attacker sends forged DNS responses — often exploiting predictable transaction IDs, the birthday-attack window, or a compromised authoritative server — that arrive before the legitimate reply. The resolver accepts and caches the fake A-record.

  Victim                DNS Resolver              Attacker
    |--- www.bank.com ---->|                          |
    |                      |<-- FORGED reply ----------|  (forged TxID)
    |                      |   www.bank.com = 10.0.0.66 (evil)
    |<-- 10.0.0.66 --------|   [cached poisoned entry]
    |--- connects to attacker's fake bank site ------>|

Consequences.

  • Credential theft — users enter passwords on a convincing fake site (pharming).
  • Man-in-the-middle / traffic interception — all traffic to the real domain flows through the attacker.
  • Malware distribution — the fake site serves drive-by downloads.
  • Denial of service / reputation damage — legitimate services become unreachable or are defamed.

Countermeasures. DNSSEC (signed, validated records), randomising source ports and transaction IDs, patching DNS servers, limiting cache TTLs, using trusted resolvers, and encryption (DoH/DoT). Note that DNS cache poisoning is distinct from on-the-wire ARP poisoning (Q16).

Answer
(a) Weak authentication leading to successful network attacks (6 marks)

Definition. Weak authentication exists when the mechanism used to verify a user's or device's identity is easy to bypass — default/blank passwords, short or guessable secrets, no MFA, credential reuse, shared accounts, or protocols that send credentials in clear text (Telnet, FTP, HTTP Basic).

How it enables attacks (mechanism).

  1. Guessing/brute force — short passwords fall to dictionary and brute-force tools; no lockout or rate limiting means unlimited attempts.
  2. Credential stuffing — passwords leaked from one site are replayed on others because users reuse them.
  3. Sniffing cleartext credentials — protocols without encryption expose passwords to a network sniffer (see Q15).
  4. Session hijacking — weak session IDs or predictable cookies let an attacker impersonate a logged-in user.
  5. Lateral movement / privilege escalation — one compromised account opens the door to more systems, especially where accounts are shared or over-privileged.

Example: an default admin/admin on a router or an SQL-injection login bypass gives the attacker an immediate authenticated foothold; from there they pivot across the network.

Countermeasures. Strong password policy and length, unique accounts, MFA, account lockout/rate limiting, hashing with salt (bcrypt/Argon2), encrypted protocols (SSH, HTTPS, TLS), session expiry, least privilege and log monitoring.

Significance. Weak authentication is one of the most common root causes of breaches (OWASP A07 / broken authentication) because it converts a remote attacker into a "legitimate" user, defeating many perimeter controls at a stroke.

(b) ARP spoofing → denial of service and countermeasures (8 marks)

Definitions. ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) maps a known IP address to an unknown MAC address on a local network and is stateless and unauthenticated. ARP spoofing/poisoning is the sending of forged ARP reply messages that associate the attacker's MAC address with another host's IP (typically the default gateway), corrupting victims' ARP caches.

 Normal:     Victim ARP cache: [gateway IP 192.168.1.1 -> Gateway MAC G]
 Poisoned:   Victim ARP cache: [gateway IP 192.168.1.1 -> Attacker MAC A]
             Gateway ARP cache:[victim IP 192.168.1.10 -> Attacker MAC A]

             Victim  -----> Attacker  -----> Gateway   (MitM / traffic interception)
             Victim  -X-> (traffic dropped by attacker)  = denial of service

How ARP spoofing leads to DoS.

  1. The attacker crafts gratuitous ARP replies claiming the gateway's IP maps to their MAC.
  2. Victims update their ARP caches with the false mapping (no authentication to stop it).
  3. All victim traffic destined off-subnet is now sent to the attacker's MAC.
  4. If the attacker simply drops the intercepted packets instead of forwarding them, victims lose internet/LAN connectivity — a denial of service.
  5. Variants: poison the gateway's cache so it sends traffic to a non-existent MAC; flood the network with forged replies; or create an ARP loop that saturates links. Repeated poisoning can also crash hosts or network equipment (resource exhaustion).

Consequences. Loss of availability (the DoS), plus confidentiality loss when the attacker forwards traffic and performs a man-in-the-middle: credential theft, session hijacking and data modification. Detection is difficult because ARP is a local, trust-based protocol.

Countermeasures.

  • Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI) with DHCP snooping — validates ARP packets against a trusted IP-to-MAC binding table (the strongest switch-based defence).
  • Static ARP entries for critical hosts (gateway/server) — prevents cache overwriting, though hard to scale.
  • Port security — limit MAC addresses per switch port to block spoofing and MAC flooding.
  • Encryption (HTTPS/TLS, IPsec, VPN) and strong authentication — make intercepted traffic useless even if redirected.
  • Detection tools — ARPWatch/arping, IDS signatures, monitoring for duplicate IPs/MACs and gratuitous ARP storms; segmented VLANs to limit scope.
Pitfall: State clearly that ARP spoofing causes DoS when packets are dropped, and becomes MitM when packets are forwarded — examiners reward this distinction.
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) give four weak-auth types, a mechanism chain, one example and the fix list. For (b) draw the ARP cache before/after diagram, number the DoS steps, separate DoS from MitM, and give four countermeasures naming DAI + DHCP snooping and port security explicitly.

(a) What is web filtering, and how does it enhance security? (6) (b) Discuss the techniques used in penetration testing for web servers and their impact on security. (8)

Answer
(a) Web filtering and how it enhances security (6 marks)

Definition. Web filtering (URL/content filtering) is the controlled blocking or allowing of access to websites and web content based on policy — categories, URLs, keywords, reputation or file types. It can run on a proxy/firewall, a dedicated appliance, a DNS filter or an endpoint agent.

How it works. When a user requests a site, the filter checks the URL/IP against category databases and rules (e.g. block "malware", "phishing", "gambling"), then allows, blocks, or warns. Filters may inspect the full URL via a proxy, or use DNS-based blocking; HTTPS is handled by certificate inspection or category-by-domain.

 User --> [Web filter / proxy] -- policy check --> allow / block / warn
                  |-- malware & phishing category DB
                  |-- logging & alerting to admin

Security enhancements.

  • Malware and phishing prevention — blocks known malicious domains and drive-by download sites before they are reached.
  • Data-loss prevention — stops uploads of sensitive data to cloud storage or email and blocks risky file types.
  • Acceptable-use enforcement — restricts non-work categories, reducing productivity loss and legal liability.
  • Bandwidth management and visibility — controls consumption and supplies logs/analytics for incident response.
  • Compliance — supports regulatory requirements to restrict harmful content.

Limitations. Over-blocking, encrypted-traffic blind spots, and bypass via VPNs/proxies or alternate DNS — so web filtering is one layer of defence-in-depth, not a complete solution.

(b) Web-server penetration-testing techniques and their impact (8 marks)

Definition. Web-server penetration testing is the authorised, controlled assessment of a web server and its applications to find exploitable weaknesses. It targets three layers: the server/OS and services, the web application, and the underlying database and configuration.

Techniques and their impact.

TechniqueWhat it tests / typical toolImpact if vulnerable

HTTP method & header analysisTRACE enabled, missing security headers, server version leakageInformation disclosure, XSS amplification, clickjacking

Testing process (aligned to OWASP, see Q11).

  1. Define scope and rules of engagement (which hosts, what test windows, no destructive acts).
  2. Passively gather info (DNS, search dorks, tech stack).
  3. Scan ports and fingerprint the server; enumerate files and directories.
  4. Map the application and test each input/parameter for injection and logic flaws.
  5. Test authentication, authorisation, session management and cryptography.
  6. Carefully validate findings (proof-of-concept, no data damage), then report with CVSS severity and remediation (patch, input validation, WAF rules, secure config).
  7. Re-test after fixes.

Overall impact on security. Done ethically, these techniques expose flaws before attackers do, letting teams patch SQLi/XSS, harden configurations, remove verbose banners, enforce TLS and add WAF rules — measurably shrinking the attack surface and supporting compliance. The same techniques in hostile hands cause data breaches, defacement, RCE and downtime — which is why authorisation and non-destructive testing are mandatory.

(a) What is buffer overflow, and what are its implications in web security? (6) (b) How can incident handling and response procedures mitigate the effects of a security breach? (8)

Answer
(a) Buffer overflow and its implications in web security (6 marks)

Definition. A buffer overflow occurs when a program writes more data into a fixed-size memory buffer than it can hold, so the excess bytes spill into adjacent memory. In languages without automatic bounds checking (C/C++), this can corrupt variables, crash the process, or — when carefully crafted — overwrite the return address to execute attacker-supplied code (arbitrary code execution).

 Stack (grows down)      Buffer overflow in action:
   [ local buffer 256 B ] <-- attacker sends 500 B
   [ saved EBP          ] <-- overwritten
   [ return address     ] <-- replaced with address of injected shellcode
   [ arguments          ]     |  (no real exploit code shown)
        ↓ when function returns → CPU jumps to attacker's code

 Types: stack overflow | heap overflow | format-string | integer overflow

Implications in web/server security.

  • Remote code execution — an untrusted HTTP parameter, header, cookie or file upload passed to a vulnerable native module/CGI can let an attacker run code as the web-server user, install a web shell or pivot deeper.
  • Denial of service — crashes the server process, taking the site offline.
  • Privilege escalation / defence bypass — especially where the vulnerable component runs as root or SYSTEM.
  • Data breach — memory disclosure can leak encryption keys and secrets.
  • Worm propagation — historic internet worms (Code Red, Blaster) spread purely via buffer-overflow exploits.

Countermeasures. Use memory-safe languages, compiler protections (stack canaries, ASLR, DEP/NX, safe libc), strict input-length validation, bounds checking, fuzzing in testing, patching, least privilege for service accounts, and a WAF/IPS as a stopgap. (No real exploit code is included, by design.)

(b) Incident handling and response to mitigate a breach (8 marks)

Definition. Incident handling/response (IR) is the organised, documented process of preparing for, detecting, analysing, containing, eradicating and recovering from a security incident, followed by lessons learned. Its goal is to limit damage and restore normal operations quickly with evidence preserved. NIST SP 800-61 and ISO 27035 are the standard references.

The six-phase incident response lifecycle.

  1. Preparation ── 2. Detection & Analysis ── 3. Containment
        ▲                                            │
        │                                            ▼
  6. Lessons Learned ◄── 5. Recovery ◄── 4. Eradication
  1. Preparation — build the IR team/CSIRT, policies, playbooks, contacts, forensic tools and baselines; train staff and set up logging/monitoring before an incident.
  2. Detection & analysis — collect alerts from IDS/SIEM, logs and users; triage and validate the incident; determine scope, severity and affected assets; preserve evidence (chain of custody, disk images, memory dumps).
  3. Containment — stop the bleeding fast: isolate affected hosts/VLANs, disable compromised accounts, block malicious IPs, apply temporary rules. Prefer short-term containment then a lasting fix.
  4. Eradication — remove the root cause: delete malware, close the exploited vulnerability (patch), remove persistence (backdoors, rogue accounts), and rebuild systems from trusted images where needed.
  5. Recovery — restore systems and data from clean backups, validate integrity, return to production in monitored stages, and watch for re-infection.
  6. Lessons learned / post-mortem — document the timeline, root cause, actions and costs; update policies, controls and playbooks; report to regulators/stakeholders as required and retain evidence for legal action.

How IR mitigates breach effects.

  • Reduces dwell time and blast radius — fast containment limits data loss and lateral movement, directly lowering breach cost.
  • Preserves evidence — correct handling supports attribution, prosecution and regulatory proof (and links to non-repudiation, Q2/Q12).
  • Enables timely notification — meets GDPR/DPDP and breach-disclosure deadlines, reducing fines and reputational harm.
  • Prevents recurrence — root-cause fixes and lessons-learned close the security gap that allowed the breach.
  • Maintains business continuity and trust — structured recovery restores services and reassures customers.

Supporting practices. Clear communication plan, legal/HR involvement, regular tabletop exercises, and integration with BCP/DR. Common pitfalls are panicking and destroying evidence (rebooting/wiping before imaging), poor documentation, and skipping the lessons-learned phase.

Pitfall: Do not jump straight from detection to recovery — containment and eradication must come first, and evidence must be preserved before any cleanup.
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) define, show the stack diagram, name 3 implications and the protection list (canary/ASLR/DEP + bounds checking). For (b) draw the six-phase cycle, explain each phase in one or two lines, then list five "mitigation" benefits. Mentioning NIST/ISO and chain of custody earns distinction marks.

(a) How can attackers manipulate event logs to avoid detection? (6) (b) Discuss the various methods used to escalate privileges in a compromised system. (8)

Answer
(a) Manipulating event logs to avoid detection (6 marks)

Definition. Log manipulation (log tampering / anti-forensics) is the altering, deleting or suppression of audit records so that an intruder's activity leaves no usable trail. It attacks the accountability and non-repudiation services and is a hallmark of advanced persistent threats that "live off the land."

Methods used.

  1. Deleting/clearing logs — emptying whole logs (e.g. wevtutil cl on Windows, truncating /var/log/auth.log) or deleting log files.
  2. Selective editing — removing only the attacker's own events so the log still looks "normal" and does not raise suspicion.
  3. Timestamp manipulation (timestomping) — changing file/event times to blend in or to confuse timeline analysis.
  4. Log flooding / noise — generating vast numbers of harmless events to bury the real events and exhaust the SIEM/storage (log smashing).
  5. Disabling or redirecting logging — turning off audit policies, killing the logging service, or changing the log destination so events never reach the collector.
  6. Tampering with forwarded logs — intercepting syslog traffic in transit or altering the local agent before it ships events off-host.
 Clean log:   [login OK][failed login][new admin user][file access]
 Attacker removes only the incriminating lines:
 Tampered:    [login OK][file access]        ← looks innocent, not empty

Why it works / impact. Many systems log locally by default, let admins delete logs, and lack integrity protection — so a compromised admin can erase evidence, blocking detection, incident response and prosecution. Countermeasures are covered in Q20 (centralised write-once logging, time sync, file integrity monitoring, SIEM alerting on log clearing).

(b) Methods used to escalate privileges in a compromised system (8 marks)

Definition. Privilege escalation is the act of obtaining higher access rights than authorised — moving from a standard user to admin/root (vertical) or from one user to another with equal rights (horizontal). It is a critical post-exploitation step because a foothold alone rarely reaches the attacker's goals.

Methods and vectors.

  1. OS/kernel and software vulnerabilities — unpatched local kernel flaws or privileged services; exploit modules (e.g. Linux Dirty COW-style bugs, Windows privilege escalation exploits) elevate the process token.
  2. Misconfigured sudo / SUID binaries — sudo -l revealing over-broad rules or NOPASSWD entries; world-writable scripts or SUID binaries that can be abused to spawn a root shell (GTFOBins-style technique, described conceptually).
  3. Stored/plaintext credentials — passwords in config files, browser caches, unattended install files, registry/autologon keys, or connection strings; also /etc/shadow hash dumping for offline cracking.
  4. Token impersonation / credential theft — Mimikatz-style memory scraping of plaintext passwords and Kerberos tickets, token stealing, pass-the-hash, pass-the-ticket.
  5. Weak service permissions & scheduled tasks — services running as SYSTEM with modifiable binaries, unquoted service paths, writable cron/task directories.
  6. Application/database flaws — SQL injection reaching sa/root, command injection in a web app, or a vulnerable setuid web module.
  7. Kernel/Driver and DLL hijacking — loading a malicious library into a privileged process via insecure search paths or writable directories.
  8. Account/group abuse — adding a user to the Administrators/sudo group using already-compromised privileged credentials, or abusing domain trust/Group Policy.

Typical attacker workflow.

 Foothold (low-priv user)
   → enumerate OS, users, groups, sudo, SUID, services, patches
   → match a weakness (missing patch / misconfig / stored credential)
   → run privilege-escalation technique
   → gain admin/root → dump more creds → disable defences → lateral move
   → cover tracks by manipulating logs (part a)

Countermeasures. Principle of least privilege and just-in-time admin, prompt patching, removing unnecessary SUID bits and writable service paths, strong credential hygiene with LSA protection/credential guard, MFA, application control/allow-listing, UAC, auditing of privilege use and group changes, endpoint detection, and separating administrative accounts from daily-use accounts (tiered administration).

Pitfall: Always define vertical vs horizontal and give examples from at least three categories (patchable vulnerability, misconfiguration, stolen credential) — a list of exploit names alone will not earn full marks.
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) list six log-manipulation methods and explain why selective editing beats wiping (looks normal). For (b) give the definition, eight numbered methods, the escalation workflow diagram and the countermeasure set. Linking (a) and (b) as "cover tracks after escalation" shows the full attack chain.

(a) What are the defenses used to protect log and accounting files from tampering? (6) (b) How can incident responders detect and respond to log and event file manipulation during an investigation? (8)

Answer
(a) Defences protecting log and accounting files from tampering (6 marks)

Definition. Log/accounting files are the audit trail used to prove who did what and when. Defences against tampering are technical and administrative controls that keep these records complete, accurate, authentic and available as evidence.

Key defences.

  1. Centralised, remote logging — forward events immediately to a dedicated, hardened SIEM/log server so local deletion cannot erase the remote copy.
  2. Write-once / append-only storage (WORM) and immutable snapshots — logs cannot be edited or deleted after writing; object-lock/retention policies enforce this.
  3. Access control and separation of duties — restrict who can read/change logs; administrators should not be able to delete their own audit records; use role-based access.
  4. Integrity protection — hashing, digital signatures and file-integrity monitoring (e.g. Tripwire, AIDE, auditd rules) detect any unauthorised change.
  5. Time synchronisation — NTP/PTP and reliable timestamps (with time-stamping authorities) prevent timestomping and keep a consistent, trustworthy timeline.
  6. Redundancy and secure backup — multiple collectors, off-site encrypted backups and tested restore procedures guarantee availability.
  7. Monitoring and alerting — SIEM rules that fire on log-clearing events, gaps in sequence numbers, service stops or missing heartbeats.
 Host A ──syslog/TLS──> [Central SIEM: append-only + hashed + NTP time]
 Host B ──syslog/TLS──>        ▲ attacker deleting local logs changes nothing
                               └─ alert on log gap / service stop / event ID 1102

Supporting measures. Encryption of logs in transit and at rest, strict retention/handling policy, regular auditing of the logging pipeline, and legal provisions making log alteration an offence. Together these uphold non-repudiation (Q2/Q12).

(b) Detecting and responding to log/event manipulation during an investigation (8 marks)

Definition. Detection and response to log manipulation is the forensic process of noticing that audit records were altered or suppressed and then preserving evidence, reconstructing the truth and restoring trustworthy logging during an incident investigation.

Detection techniques — what responders look for.

  • Gaps and inconsistencies — missing event sequence numbers, time gaps, or a suspiciously "clean" log after known activity; events out of chronological order.
  • Tell-tale clearing events — Windows Security log cleared (Event ID 1102), audit service stopped (1100), Linux log rotation anomalies, or unexpected truncation.
  • Correlate independent sources — compare host logs with SIEM/network flow, firewall, proxy, DNS, EDR telemetry and backup copies; a discrepancy reveals tampering.
  • File-integrity checks — verify hashes/signatures against baselines; check file modification times and permissions (timestomping detection).
  • Anomalies in log behaviour — missing heartbeats, sudden drop in event volume, duplicate or forged event IDs, unusual log file size.

Response procedure (forensically sound).

  1. Declare and scope — treat suspected log tampering as an incident; record the discovery, who found it and the affected systems.
  2. Preserve evidence first — acquire forensic images of disks and memory before rebooting or cleaning; capture volatile data; maintain a documented chain of custody with hashes. Do not "fix" logs before imaging.
  3. Isolate and contain — isolate affected hosts, disable compromised accounts and revoke tokens to stop further alteration.
  4. Reconstruct events — correlate surviving logs with network/EDR/DNS/proxy records, backups and remote collectors; build an accurate timeline using normalised time.
  5. Eradicate and restore logging — remove the attacker's persistence, patch the entry point, re-enable and harden logging (central forwarding, WORM, tighter ACLs, NTP), and verify events flow again.
  6. Document and report — record the tampering as evidence of intent, brief legal/compliance and regulators where required, and preserve records for prosecution.
  7. Lessons learned — update detection rules (alert on log clearing/gaps), close the security gap, and exercise the response plan.

Challenges. Encrypted logs, missing baselines, attacker-controlled time sources and the sheer volume of data make reconstruction hard — which is why prevention (part a) and cross-source correlation are essential. Log tampering itself is strong evidence of an attacker's attempt to hide activity and should escalate the incident's severity.

Pitfall: Never clean or reboot a system before capturing volatile evidence — responders often destroy the very proof they need. Always say "image first, analyse on a copy."
Exam tip (both parts): For (a) list seven defences with centralised/WORM logging, integrity hashing, NTP and access control as the big four. For (b) split cleanly into detection signals (gaps, Event ID 1102, correlation, hashes) and a numbered response that puts evidence preservation before containment/eradication. Mentioning chain of custody and cross-source correlation earns distinction marks.
