01

How questions are set in this course

Read the examiner

Most questions in Industrial Safety fall into a small number of shapes. Spotting the shape before you write stops you from answering the wrong thing and saves time:

  • Define a term — give the meaning in one clear sentence, then add one line that shows you know what it implies (what it protects, what breaks it, or one concrete example).
  • List the categories / types / steps — ordered and enumerated, with a short note per item so it reads as a structured list, not a dump of headings.
  • Compare two things — the examiner is checking you know the boundary between them. A two-column table with the same axes for both is the cleanest way to show it, and it is faster to write than two separate paragraphs.
  • Describe a process / a plant / a technique — narrative, step by step, from start to outcome, with the "why" at each step. For plant layouts (gasifier, biogas plant, tidal basin), name the flow and what each component does.
  • Discuss advantages and limitations — both sides, labelled. A one-sided answer loses marks; the limitation is often where the marks are.
  • "With a neat diagram" / "labelled sketch" — labels matter more than artistry. A labelled schematic that shows the flow or layout the question names scores; a pretty but unlabelled one does not. The diagrams built into each module page are exactly the shapes examiners expect — use them as the reference when you redraw from memory.
The most useful reflex: rephrase the question in plain English before you write. "Explain gasification" really means "what is it, how does it work, what comes out, what are the two common types, one advantage and one limitation." If you answer each of those, you have covered the mark scheme.
02

Core definitions to keep ready

Part A / Part A-like
TermWhat to say (short, precise)
AccidentAn unplanned event that results in injury and/or damage — something that actually happened, not a possibility.
Reportable accidentAn accident of a type and severity the law requires to be reported to the appropriate authority — the threshold is defined by statute, not by opinion.
Dangerous occurrenceA near-miss or specified event that could have caused serious harm (e.g. collapse, explosion) but did not — still reportable in many systems because it signals a real hazard.
Unsafe actA human action that departs from a safe procedure — e.g. bypassing a guard, working at height without fall protection.
Unsafe conditionA physical hazard present in the workplace — e.g. unguarded blade, slippery floor, exposed live conductor.
HazardA source or situation with the potential to cause harm — not harm itself, but the thing that could cause it.
RiskLikelihood × severity of harm — how probable an unwanted event is, times how bad it would be.
PPEPersonal protective equipment — the last line of defence, worn by the worker (helmet, respirator, boot, glove, etc.).
Confined spaceAn enclosed or partially enclosed space not meant for continuous occupancy, with limited entry/exit and a realistic risk of hazardous atmosphere, engulfment, or entrapment.
GasificationThermochemical conversion of solid biomass into a combustible gas (CO + H₂, plus some CH₄) through partial combustion with restricted air — no solid carbon residue in the ideal case.
BiogasAn inflammable methane-rich gas produced by anaerobic digestion of cattle dung and other organic matter in a digester.
Hazard identificationThe systematic search for sources of harm before they cause an incident — the basis for HIRA, HAZOP, and similar methods.
HAZOPHazard and Operability study — a structured team method that takes a process node, picks a parameter, applies a guide-word, and works causes → consequences → safeguards → recommendations.
MSDSMaterial Safety Data Sheet — the document that tells a worker what a chemical is, how it harms, how to handle and store it, and what to do in an emergency.
When a 3-mark "define" question appears, say the meaning in one sentence and add one line that shows you know what it implies — that second line is often where the third mark comes from.
03

Safety-performance rates — how they work

Formulae + meaning
IndicatorHow it is builtWhat it tells you
Frequency rate (FR)Number of injuries × base (e.g. 10⁵ or 10⁶) ÷ total man-hours workedHow often injuries happen relative to exposure — good for comparing activity levels, but hides rare devastating events.
Severity rate (SR)Days lost × base ÷ total man-hours workedHow bad the injuries are on average — captures the cost in lost time.
Incidence rate (IR)Cases (or lost-time cases) per given workforce or per man-hoursHow many people are actually getting hurt — a workforce-oriented view.
Activity rateExposed / active workers ÷ total workforceWhat fraction of the workforce is in the hazard zone — useful for scoping the exposure.
Use at least two together. Comparing only frequency can hide a severe but rare accident; comparing only severity can hide a high-frequency minor-injury problem. A real comparison of two industries or two periods should mention both, and say which you think is the bigger concern and why.

05

How to match your answer to the marks

Marks-aware
MarksWhat the examiner expectsWhat to write
3 A tight definition or a short list — enough to show you know the term, not a full essay. One sentence of meaning, then one line with an angle (what it protects, how it's done, one example, or one limitation). Stop when you have said the thing.
7 A comparison, a short process, or "list with notes." Three to five headed points, each with a one-line reason. If it is a comparison, use a small table. A worked example or mini-numeric step helps here.
14 A full answer: definition, principle, sub-points, a comparison or diagram if asked, and both advantages and limitations. Lead with the definition, then build structured sub-sections (a/b/c or a table), then draw the labelled diagram the question names if it asks for one, then close with limitations and a concrete example. Split long answers into (a)/(b) if that helps.
Don't pad. Repeating the same point in different words wastes time and reads as uncertainty. If the question is 3 marks, a clean 3-mark answer beats a 14-mark answer that wanders.
06

Do's and don'ts in the exam

Practical

Do

  • Rephrase the question in plain English before you write.
  • Lead a long answer with a one-line definition or framing.
  • Use a two-column table when a question asks to "compare" or two things are naturally paired.
  • Give one concrete example, numeric step, or plant component name — it anchors a mark a bare list often misses.
  • If the question asks for advantages and limitations, give both, labelled.
  • If it asks for a labelled diagram, draw the one from the module page — labels are what score.

Don't

  • Pad with headings you can't explain — a misused term reads as guessing.
  • Answer a "compare" as two separate lists with no connecting axis.
  • Stop at the advantages when the question asks for limitations too.
  • Run past the marks — a 3-mark answer should not be half a page.
  • Leave a diagram out because you cannot draw well — labels on a schematic are what score, not artistry.
  • Mix up pairs that are frequently swapped (updraft/downdraft, KVIC/Janata, PAFC/PEM, fixed/open OTEC) — those are exactly where a trick question lives.
07

How to use these notes

Study flow

Read a module page top to bottom once to get the shape — the headings are the skeleton, the tables are the comparisons, and the worked examples are the parts that usually earn marks. Then:

  • For definitions: use the core-definitions table on this page (02) as a checklist — can you say each one in one sentence? If not, that is a gap to close before the exam.
  • For comparisons: rehearse the recurring pairs in section 04 from memory — write the table out once, then close the notes and try again.
  • For processes and plant: narrate them aloud in your own words, step by step, with the "why" at each step. If you cannot name what comes out of a gasifier, what a biogas dome does, or what the ammonia loop in OTEC is for, go back to the module page.
  • For "labelled diagram": redraw the diagram on the module page from memory — gasifier zones, KVIC/Janata biogas, OTEC loop, tidal basin, wind turbine, fuel cell, machine guard, fire-class wheel, HAZOP flow. Labels matter more than neatness.
  • For full answers: use the question bank on papers.html as practice — try one, then read the model answer and note what it included that you missed.
The goal is not to memorise this whole site word-for-word. It is to be able to rebuild each module's key tables and a labelled diagram from memory, define each core term in one sentence, and narrate each process and attack in your own words with the "why" attached.
