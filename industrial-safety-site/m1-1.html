Safety Introduction

Why safety matters and how it pays back in productivity, the core definitions every investigator uses, the major theories of accident causation, and how a safety organization is built — management, supervisors, workers, unions, government, and voluntary agencies — plus the safety policy, safety officer, and safety committee.

Definitions Accident theories Safety org
01

Need for Safety & Safety–Productivity

Why

Every year millions of occupational / industrial accidents occur, losing production time equal to millions of man-hours and machine-hours. About one-fifth of that loss comes from those actually injured (temporary or permanent disablement); the rest is fellow workers helping the injured and dealing with the damage. Deaths make the loss alarming. The conclusion: identify accident causes and act to control them.

There are direct costs (machine downtime, damage to equipment, compensation, legal implications) and indirect costs (idle time of nearby equipment, loss of morale, disruption). Safety measures avoid these and meet a moral responsibility toward workmen.

Safety and productivity are linked, not competing. Best-in-class performers achieve higher Overall Equipment Effectiveness, less unscheduled downtime, and less than half the injury rate of average performers. A safe workplace is a productive one — accidents drain time, morale, and money; prevention pays.
02

Core Definitions

Memorise
TermMeaning
AccidentAn unplanned event that results in injury, damage, or loss — one factor in a chain that may lead to harm.
InjuryPhysical harm (temporary or permanent disablement) to a person caused by an accident.
Unsafe actA human action that deviates from a safe procedure and creates a hazard (e.g. bypassing a guard, not using PPE).
Unsafe conditionA physical hazard in the workplace (e.g. unguarded machine, slippery floor, faulty wiring) that can cause an accident.
Dangerous occurrenceA near-miss / specific dangerous event that did not cause injury but had the potential to (e.g. collapse, explosion, release of toxic gas).
Reportable accidentAn accident that, by statute, must be reported to the enforcing authority (factories / mines act) — typically those causing death, serious injury, or dangerous occurrence.
Note the chain: an unsafe act or unsafe condition + an accident → an injury. Controlling the first two prevents the rest.
Where accidents come from: accident data shows the large majority arise from unsafe acts (~60%) and a further large share from unsafe working conditions (~38%), with the small remainder from environmental and personal factors. So behaviour-based control (training, supervision, PPE discipline) plus fixing physical conditions together address most accidents.
03

Theories of Accident Causation

CO1

Accident theories guide investigations — they tell you how far back to look for root causes. The major ones:

TheoryCore idea
1. Domino Theory (H. W. Heinrich)An accident is one factor in a sequence — visualize dominoes standing on edge. When one falls, the chain reaction proceeds to an injury. Remove any one domino (e.g. the unsafe act) and the injury is prevented. Foundation: social environment / ancestry → personal fault → unsafe act/condition → accident → injury.
2. Human-Factors / Epidemiological TheoryAn accident is a function of a predisposition (personal/background factors) and a triggering factor (the immediate hazard) — analogous to disease causation. Control either the host (person) or the agent (hazard).
3. Systems TheoryAn accident results from failures in a cause–system, not a single bad actor. Safety is a sub-system of the organization; improve the system (design, procedures, feedback) to prevent accidents.
4. Energy-Release Theory (W. Haddon)Injury occurs when harmful energy is transferred to the body faster than it can be absorbed. Control by preventing the release, blocking the transfer path, or strengthening the target (person).
5. Combination / Behaviour TheoryAccidents arise from a combination of factors (personal + environmental); behaviour-based safety changes the acts that lead to them.
Why it matters in the exam: Heinrich's Domino and Haddon's Energy-Release are the two most-cited. Be ready to explain "remove a domino" and "control the energy transfer path."
04

Safety Organization

Structure

A safety organization has objectives (prevent accidents, comply with law, promote a safety culture), types (line vs staff safety functions), and functions (inspection, training, investigation, standards). Everyone has a role:

RoleResponsibility in safety
ManagementSet policy, provide resources, define accountability, lead by example, review performance. Safety is a line-management responsibility.
SupervisorsEnforce safe practices on the floor, coach workers, report hazards, investigate near-misses, ensure PPE use.
WorkmenFollow procedures, use PPE, report unsafe conditions, take part in safety suggestions.
UnionsRepresent worker safety interests, participate in committees, promote safe practices.
GovernmentLegislate (Factories/Mines Acts), inspect, enforce, publish standards and codes of practice.
Voluntary agenciesNGOs / professional bodies (e.g. safety councils) that train, publish guidance, and raise awareness.
Assignment of duties (who does what): safety responsibility is assigned down the line — the project manager owns overall safety and resources; the site engineer implements safe methods; the safety manager / safety officer plans, inspects, trains and investigates; supervisors enforce on the floor; and workmen follow procedures and report hazards. Each level has a written assignment so accountability is clear.
05

Safety Policy, Officer & Committee

Governance

Safety policy is the organization's documented commitment — it assigns responsibility and authority for safety, defines procedures (measurement, inspection, correction, investigation), and makes clear that safety is a management function, not an afterthought.

Safety Officer — responsibilities: develop and implement the safety program, inspect workplaces, investigate accidents, train employees, advise management, and supervise everyone working under them to ensure safe performance. Authority: power to stop unsafe work and to require corrective action.

Safety committee — a joint body (management + workers) that meets regularly to review safety performance, investigate incidents, and recommend improvements. It typically keeps meeting minutes and an attendance register, and follows up on the actions it assigns.

Need / types / advantages: a committee gives safety a permanent forum, surfaces floor-level hazards that management misses, improves communication, and builds shared ownership of safety results. Types range from a statutory plant-level committee to departmental or joint (union–management) committees.
