Personal Protection in the Work Environment

Personal protective equipment — its types (respiratory and non-respiratory), the standards that govern it, the safety-performance rates used to measure an organisation's record, the discipline of housekeeping and 5S, the work-permit system, confined-space entry, and lockout/tagout energy isolation. All content is drawn from the lecture source; nothing is invented.

01

Types of PPE

CO2

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) means any device or appliance designed to be worn or held by an individual for protection against one or more health and safety hazards. It includes safety glasses, gloves, face shields, protective clothing or footwear. PPE is the last line of defence: it is considered a last resort because it only protects the individual worker, is prone to failure or misuse (e.g. wearing the wrong RPE for the job), and can give a false sense of security. It does not eliminate the hazard at the source — if the equipment fails, the worker is exposed. Any item of PPE imposes a barrier between the wearer and the environment, which can create strain, impair work and cause discomfort; good ergonomic design minimises these barriers.

PPE is split into simple (basic design, protects against lower-risk hazards; the user can see the risk rising and decide to wear it) and complex (technical design, protects against fatal or serious risk where the user cannot identify the hazard in time). About 0–40% of total accidents can be prevented or controlled by proper PPE use. It defends against toxic exposure, oxygen deficiency, dust, chemical splashes, steam, water, flying particles, hot substances, radiation, sharp edges, welding/cutting/grinding, glare, falls, falling bodies, noise, scrap cleaning, material handling, pipeline opening, electric shock, burns and firefighting.

The need for PPE exists because: (1) engineering controls, materials, process, equipment and safety devices can fail, and PPE then acts as a barrier; (2) some operations or accidents make engineering controls impractical — repair, maintenance, toxic/oxygen-deficient atmospheres, work at height, welding, cutting, grinding, chipping; (3) it avoids contact with dangerous substances, noise, vibration and radiation; (4) it protects from atmospheric contaminants; (5) providing suitable PPE is a legal as well as moral duty.

Respiratory Protective Equipment (RPE)

RPE protects the wearer against inhaling hazardous substances. Employers must first attempt to eliminate the hazard at source; RPE is used only after all other reasonably practicable controls. It is divided into:

  • Respirator (filtering device) — uses filters to remove contaminants:
    • Non-powered (relies on wearer's breathing): disposable filtering half mask, half mask, full face mask
    • Powered (motor passes air through filter): half mask, full face mask, helmets, hoods, visors
  • Breathing apparatus (BA) — needs breathing-quality air from an independent source (air cylinder or compressor)

Facepieces are tight-fitting (need a good seal — filtering facepieces, half/full-face masks) or loose-fitting (rely on enough clean air; hoods, helmets, visors, blouses, suits — only as powered respirators or BA). WARNING: only BA is suitable for use in oxygen-deficient atmospheres. RPE not worn or selected appropriately is totally ineffective and gives a false sense of protection.

Non-respiratory PPE

  • Eye protection (e.g. goggles) — against machines, lasers, impacts, heat, tools, flying particles/dust, electrical work, chemical handling
  • Head protection (e.g. safety helmets) — industrial safety helmets (falling objects), bump caps (bumping; not for falling objects), firefighter helmets
  • Ear protection (e.g. earplugs) — noise over 85 dB causes loss; protection required at 90 dB
  • Foot protection (e.g. steel toecap boots) — safety boots/shoes, wellington boots
  • Hand & arm protection (e.g. gloves) — gloves, gloves with cuffs, gauntlets/sleeves, arm protection
  • Body / skin protection (e.g. high-visibility clothing) — separates, aprons, overalls, high-vis jackets/tops/trousers/vests
  • Fall protection (e.g. safety harnesses) — work restraint and work positioning systems

Eye protection comes in three types: safety glasses (good for flying objects head-on, not vapours/dust), goggles (protect from all angles, rim contacts face), and face shields/visors (whole-face, worn over prescription glasses). Foot injuries are 5% of disabling workplace injuries; workers not wearing safety shoes account for 75% of occupational foot injuries. Hand/finger injuries are 18% of disabling injuries and ~25% of industrial accidents.

PPE only works if selected and cared for: choose the right type for the specific hazard (e.g. tight-fitting RPE needs a good face seal; BA only for oxygen-deficient air), inspect it before every use, clean and disinfect after use, store it in a clean dry place away from contamination and sunlight, and replace it when cracked, perforated, degraded or past its service life. PPE that is the wrong type, damaged, or poorly fitted gives a false sense of safety.
02

Monitoring Safety Performance

Rates

Safety performance is measured with four rates. The first three are expressed per million man-hours (10⁶) in the source's worked examples — some literature uses 10⁵; the formulas below follow the source's examples.

RateWhat it measuresFormula (per source)
Frequency rate (FR)Number of lost-time (disabling) injuries per million man-hours worked — how often injuries occur\( \dfrac{\text{Lost-time injuries}\times 10^{6}}{\text{man-hours worked}} \)
Severity rate (SR)Man-days lost per million man-hours worked — how serious injuries are\( \dfrac{\text{Days lost}\times 10^{6}}{\text{man-hours worked}} \)
Incidence rate (IR)Lost-time injuries per thousand persons employed\( \dfrac{\text{Lost-time injuries}\times 1000}{\text{persons employed}} \)

Frequency rate = (No. of lost-time injuries × 10⁶) ÷ man-hours worked Severity rate = (days lost in year due to accidents × 10⁶) ÷ man-hours worked Incidence rate = (lost-time injuries × 1000) ÷ persons employed
Worked example — Frequency rate. Workers = 500; disabling injuries/year = 5; average hours/worker/year = 2000. \(\text{Man-hours} = 500\times 2000 = 1{,}000{,}000.\) FR = 5 ÷ 1,000,000 × 1,000,000 = 5.
Worked example — Severity rate. Workers = 2000; days lost = 100; hours/worker/year = 2000. For a cut fingertip, add the standard 35 days → 135. SR = 135 ÷ (2000 × 2000) × 1,000,000 = 135 ÷ 4 = 33.75. For a total-disability case add 6000 days → 6100; SR = 6100 ÷ 4 = 1525.
Definitions & the 10⁶ basis. Man-hours worked = total employee-hours by all employees (managerial, supervisory, professional, technical, clerical, contractors), from payroll/time-clock including overtime; if not feasible, estimate man-days × hours/day. Disabling (Lost-Time) Injury = an injury causing disablement beyond the shift on which it occurred. Reportable Disabling Injury = one causing death or disablement to the extent prescribed by statute. Why 1,000,000? 500 workers × 8 h/day × 5 days/week × 50 weeks = 1,000,000 man-hours/year — the reference denominator. Activity rate emphasises the cost of accident-prevention activities against the cost of accidents.
03

Housekeeping & 5S

Discipline

Effective housekeeping controls or eliminates workplace hazards; poor housekeeping frequently contributes to incidents. It is not just cleanliness — it includes keeping work areas neat and orderly, maintaining halls and floors free of slip/trip hazards, and removing waste and fire hazards. It is an ongoing operation, not a one-time "panic" cleanup (which is costly and ineffective).

Responsibility — management: train employees to recognise hazardous conditions (wet floors, messy floors, equipment left out, improper lifting); prepare and communicate a safety manual at induction; post safety rules; run continuous training; keep a preventive-maintenance programme; include safety inspection in the supervisor's checklist; store toxic chemicals in closed, labelled cupboards; keep waste containers leak-proof; keep fire-exit stairs clear; use signs such as "Wet Floor". Employees: maintain their own area daily and follow the rules.

Advantages of good housekeeping: reduced handling / easier material flow; fewer tripping and slipping incidents; decreased fire hazards; lower worker exposure to dusts and vapours; better control of tools/materials and inventory; more efficient cleanup and maintenance; better hygiene and health; more effective use of space; reduced property damage via preventive maintenance; less janitorial work; improved morale; improved productivity (tools easy to find).

04

Work Permit System

Control

The work permit specifies the conditions and procedures for the safe execution of work and allows it to be carried out under controlled-risk conditions. It is a legal document between issuer and executor for commencement of a job in a hazardous area.

Objectives: (1) only authorised persons work in a clearly notified hazardous area; (2) the permitted person is aware of the safety issues and knows the necessary precautions have been taken; (3) it is the legal document between issuer and executor; (4) it distributes equal responsibility for the job. In short — don't do anything without a proper work permit.

Hot work permit (Red)

Required for any job involving a source of ignition, spark or fire — welding, drilling, grinding, riveting, cutting, use of internal-combustion engines. Considers flammable/combustible materials and combustible gas in the vicinity. Requires fire-watch, flammable-gas checks and a fire extinguisher.

Cold work permit (Green)

Required for hazardous maintenance work that does not involve ignition, spark or fire. Issued after a risk assessment finds no flammable or explosive risk. Covers non-ignition high-risk work such as confined space, electrical and lifting, specifying isolation, LOTO and competency.

Typical industrial methodology: identify the job and its hazards → assess risk → define controls and isolation → authorise (permit issuer + receiver sign-off) → periodic monitoring → close-out and cancellation. Many Indian industries use standardised permit forms with multiple sign-offs; the system assigns clear responsibilities and ensures operating personnel know what is happening, the precautions required, and that the persons executing understand the nature and extent of the hazards.

05

Entry into Confined Spaces

High risk

A confined space is a fully or partially enclosed space that: is not primarily designed for continuous human occupancy; has limited or restricted entrance/exit that can complicate rescue or evacuation; and risks health and safety due to its design, contents, work activities, or mechanical/process/safety hazards. It need not be small — examples include silos, vats, hoppers, tanks, pits, sewers, pipes, boilers, manholes, digesters, rail tank cars and storage bins; ditches/wells/trenches with limited access also count.

Hazards: poor air quality (oxygen deficiency below 19.5% — simple asphyxiants such as argon, nitrogen, carbon monoxide — or enrichment above 23%); toxic gases; fire/explosion (flammable liquids/gases, combustible dust); chemical, process, physical (noise, heat/cold, radiation, electrical, lighting), safety (moving parts, engulfment, falls), biological (bacteria, mould) and traffic hazards.

Procedure before entry:

  • Confirm the confined-space hazard assessment and control programme has been followed; a trained, experienced person identifies and evaluates all existing and potential hazards, inside and outside.
  • Air-quality testing from outside, side-to-side and top-to-bottom, by a trained worker with calibrated remote-probe equipment: oxygen within safe limits, no toxic/flammable atmosphere, ventilation operating. Record results on the Entry Permit. Use continuous monitoring where conditions can change.
  • Ventilation: natural ventilation is unreliable — mechanical ventilation (blowers/fans) is usually necessary; ensure clean air throughout, watch for trapped pockets, exhaust away from outside workers, and never substitute oxygen for fresh air (it raises fire/explosion risk).
  • Energy isolation: all hazardous energy (electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical, thermal) must be de-energised/isolated and locked out; disconnect pipes physically or bolt isolation blanks (closing valves is not enough); use two blocking valves with a bleed between them.
  • Fire/explosion control for hot work: remove all flammables first; keep flammable concentration below 10% of the Lower Explosive Limit and oxygen between 19.5% and 23%; clean/shield combustible coatings; spark-resistant, bonded/grounded tools; no fuel containers inside.
  • Permit + stand-by attendant + rescue plan; PPE/respirators, hearing protection as needed; beware that PPE can increase heat and reduce mobility.
Never enter a confined space on assumption — determine if it is one, assess hazards, test and ventilate, isolate energy (LOTO), permit, watch and plan rescue.
04b

Lockout / Tagout (LOTO) — Energy Isolation

Life-saving control

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) is the procedure used to make sure a machine or circuit cannot be energised while someone is servicing it. It is the control referenced whenever a permit says "isolation" — it removes hazardous energy (electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal) and physically prevents re-energisation.

Lockout = apply a lock (padlock/valve lock) so the energy-isolating device cannot be moved. Tagout = attach a warning tag ("Do not operate") when a lock cannot be applied; the tag alone is a warning, not a physical block, so lockout is preferred where possible.

Standard sequence: notify affected workers → shut down normally → isolate each energy source at its disconnect → apply locks/tags (each worker their own) → release stored energy (springs, capacitors, pressure) → verify zero energy (try start, confirm dead) → work → on completion, remove only your own lock and restore in reverse order. Each lock has one key, held by the person who applied it.

Why it matters: unplanned re-energisation during maintenance is a leading cause of crush, electrocution, and amputation injuries. LOTO is the single most important control for safe maintenance of guarded machines (see Module 4) and confined-space entry.
