Safety Hazards in Machines & Material Handling

Mechanical machinery hazards and the principle of machine guarding (point-of-operation, guard types and devices); safety in turning and grinding; gas and arc welding/cutting precautions; manual and mechanical material handling with equipment maintenance; and the hearing conservation program for production industries.

01

Machine Safeguarding

CO4

Moving machinery causes injuries in many ways: people are struck by moving parts or ejected material; parts of the body are drawn in or trapped between rollers, belts and pulley drives; sharp edges cut or sever and pointed parts stab or puncture the skin; rough surfaces cause friction or abrasion; people are crushed between parts, or between a part and a fixed structure, while shearing occurs where two parts move past one another; emissions (steam, water, electricity) burn or shock; and faults from improper use add further risk. Not only powered machinery is hazardous — a hand-operated guillotine or fly press can still injure if not properly safeguarded. Machine guards are the first line of defense, protecting operators from ingoing nip points, rotating parts, sparks and flying debris.

Mechanical machinery hazards & control

HazardWhat happensTypical control

Cutting / SeveringContact with a moving sharp-edged part, a rotating part, or any moving part (bandsaw, drill, lathe).Guarded blades; secured chucks/bits; gloves.

Principles of machine guarding

  • Prevent contact: the safeguard must prevent hands, arms or any body part from reaching dangerous moving parts.
  • Secure: not easily removed or tampered with; made of durable material and firmly fixed to the machine.
  • Protect from falling objects: nothing may fall into moving parts — a dropped tool can become a projectile.
  • Create no new hazards: no shear point, jagged edge or unfinished surface that could lacerate; guard edges rolled or bolted.
  • Create no interference: a guard that slows the job will be overridden; good guarding improves efficiency by removing fear of injury.
  • Allow safe lubrication: oil reservoirs located outside the guard with a line to the point, so lubrication needs no entry into the hazard area.

Point-of-operation guarding

Every machine has three areas: the point of operation (where work is performed — cutting, shaping, boring, forming), the power transmission apparatus (flywheels, pulleys, belts, couplings, cams, spindles, chains, cranks, gears), and the operating controls (a mechanical/electrical control to cut power from the point of operation). A solid guard prevents hands reaching the point of operation; where a solid guard would impede productivity, optical sensing such as safety light curtains acts as the safeguard.

Types of guards vs devices

Five general safeguarding methods exist — guards (barriers), devices (control/withdrawal), automated feeding & ejection, machine location/distance, and miscellaneous aids (chip/spark shields, holding tools, awareness barriers) — but the two central distinctions are guard type and device type:

Guards (barriers)

  • Fixed: permanently attached, no moving parts; encloses point of operation/flywheels; maximum protection, minimum maintenance, but blocks access for adjustment.
  • Interlocked: opens → machine power shut off/stopped before reach-in; good for clearing jams, but needs careful adjustment.
  • Adjustable: manually set and locked for different stock sizes; can be defeated if poorly adjusted.
  • Self-adjusting: opens only as far as the stock needs (table saws); off-the-shelf, but not always maximum protection.

Devices (control)

  • Presence-sensing: photoelectric (light beam broken → ram will not cycle) or electromechanical probe that blocks the cycle on obstruction.
  • Pullback: cables on the hands withdraw them as the ram descends (stroking machines).
  • Restraint (holdout): cables fix the hands within a safe area; hand-feeding tools needed.
  • Safety trip controls: pressure-sensitive body bar stops the machine in an emergency.
  • Two-hand control / trip: concurrent both-hand actuation keeps hands out of the danger zone.
Before starting any machine: check it is complete with all safeguards fitted and free from defects; produce a safe system of work covering set-up, use, blockages, repairs and planned maintenance; ensure static machines are fixed down; choose the right machine; and confirm new machines are CE marked with a Declaration of Conformity. Isolate/lock-off before clearing blockages, cleaning or adjusting.
02

Safety in Turning & Grinding

Operations

Turning (lathe)

A workpiece secured in a chuck is turned against a cutting tool. Before starting: secure all stock in the chuck/mount, turn the chuck by hand to check for binding, embed the cup centre, clamp tail-stock and tool rests, and give clearance for rotating stock (small-diameter stock must not project too far without tail-stock support). In operation: do not force the tool or take too big a roughing cut; confirm carriage/cross-feed direction before auto-feed; shut off power and let the machine stop before leaving; stop on odd noise or excessive vibration. The chuck: never leave the key in the chuck — secure it before releasing; never run the chuck on/off by power; do not reverse power to stop rotation except when tapping. Speed: turn large-diameter stock at very low speed, use the correct speed/feed, and stop before adjusting or measuring.

Grinding

All grinders (pedestal, bench, free-standing, portable) are hazardous if poorly maintained — beyond flying particles, dust and sparks, a shattering abrasive wheel in motion can severely injure the user and others.

  • Provide and properly adjust guards per the manufacturer's manual; replace damaged guards (a broken rotating wheel is dangerous).
  • Check the manufacturer's stated running speed / markings and the wheel's maximum speed before use.
  • Clean and service to the manufacturer's recommendations and record all maintenance.
  • Ensure the machine will not run unattended; keep the work area floor clean and clear of flammables/combustible dust.
  • Wear safety glasses/goggles or a face shield (with glasses); gloves, apron, metatarsal boots, hearing and respiratory protection as the work requires.
  • Do not use cracked or excessively vibrating wheels; do not operate on a wet surface; keep the power cord away from the wheel.
  • Never adjust the workpiece or mounting while operating; do not exceed the recommended depth of cut; remove the workpiece before turning the machine off; use proper wheel guards throughout.
03

Welding & Cutting Precautions

Gas & Arc

Gas welding / cutting

Gas welding uses acetylene (flammable; can ignite and condense) and oxygen (does not burn but makes other substances burn faster and can explode if exposed to fire). PPE: eye goggles/safety glasses against infrared; flameproof apron, gloves, cap/helmet, booth; well-fitted apron (no fraying), long sleeves and trousers covering the boots; all clothing free of oil/grease. Storage & handling: cylinders chained to prevent falling, kept upright, away from heat and flammables, in a well-ventilated area with acetylene and oxygen stored separately; valves closed before moving, regulators and caps in place, hands kept off grease/oil. Operation: flame arrestors must be fitted in both acetylene and oxygen lines — one by the low-pressure regulator and one near the torch; keep oxygen pressure higher than acetylene to stop acetylene flashback; do not use acetylene above 1 bar gauge. On backfire, close the oxygen valve first then acetylene; on flashback (explosion in the gas hose) set both cylinder valves apart and replace all hoses with new ones before continuing.

Arc welding / cutting

Arc welding uses an electric arc (AC or DC) between electrode and base metal. Equipment: proper earth/ground connection; correct switchboard and fuse; do not overload or overheat the machine; call an expert on abnormal sound; electrode-holder insulation is mandatory. Personal safety: protect eyes from infrared/ultraviolet, flying sparks, spatter, hot slag and electrode stub; fit special filter glass in helmet/head screen (opacity depends on material); wear leather apron, hand gloves, leg guards and safety boots; protect against fumes, gases and dust; know ventilation and health protection. Gear list: ear plugs/muffs when noise is high, leather boots and gloves, glasses or helmet with cover plate, respirators for toxic fumes; do not operate if clothing is wet; a person with a pacemaker is advised not to weld.

Core difference: gas welding hazards centre on flammable/explosive gases and flashback (controlled by flame arrestors and cylinder discipline); arc welding hazards centre on electric shock/earthing, intense UV/IR radiation and toxic fume (controlled by earthing, filter glass, ventilation and dry PPE).

Material Handling

Manual & Mechanical

Material handling is the movement, protection, storage and control of materials through manufacturing, warehousing, distribution and disposal — manually, semi-automated or automated, in batches or one-by-one, horizontally, vertically or both. It is broadly classified as manual handling (carried out by people — lifting, carrying, pushing, pulling, palletizing, stocking) and mechanical handling (carried out by equipment — cranes, conveyors, forklifts, hoists), with safety considerations differing for each. Equipment falls into transporting (conveyors, cranes, industrial/forklift trucks, manual trucks), storage (racking, shelving, mezzanine) and positioning (material lifts, electric chain hoists, suction lifts).

Manual handling (MMH)

MMH — lifting, lowering, pushing, pulling, carrying, holding or restraining — is the most common cause of occupational fatigue, low-back pain and lower-back injury. Hazards include strains/sprains from improper or oversized loads, fractures/bruises from being struck or caught in pinch points, and cuts from falling or badly stored materials. Steps for lifting: feet apart for a stable base; bend the knees so hands are near the waist; keep back straight, chin tucked, shoulders aligned with hips; keep arms within the legs' boundary; lift smoothly without jerk; adjust for precise positioning; give a command for group lifts. Keep lifts between shoulder and knuckle height, begin close to the body, avoid twisting/bending, and seek help when a load is too bulky to grasp, cannot be seen over, or cannot be safely handled. Restrict the load to 55 kg (Indian statute), do a trial lift, and confirm the path is clear. PPE: gloves for sharp/rough loads, eye protection, steel-toed boots, metatarsal guards.

Manual handling risk assessment

Assess the task, individual, load and environment (TILE): load (heavy, bulky, unstable, sharp), individual (strength, age, health), task (push/pull, posture, distance, repetition) and environment (space, flooring, temperature, lighting, bulky PPE). A full assessment is always required for large forward reaches, lifting below floor or above head height, handling more frequent than one lift per two minutes, torso twisting, team handling, complex activity, hard-to-grasp loads, unfavourable conditions, or loads not held against the body. For pushing/pulling the guideline limits are a 20 kg start/stop force for men (15 kg women) and 10 kg sustained (7 kg women).

Mechanical handling

Heavy loads are handled by palletizer, chain pulley block, forklifts, conveyors, cranes and hoists. All lifting machines need a valid inspection certificate by a competent person — most tools valid six months, lifting machines (including forklift) one year. Do: use tools/hoists of adequate capacity; restrict use to authorized persons; mark identification number and safe working load; check emergency/limit switches and overload alarm before starting; estimate weight, distance and hazards; barricade the lift area; pad sharp corners; use plate/beam clamps and guide ropes. Don't: use faulty tackles or hoists/slings/chains that have lost more than 10% of breaking strength or carry a missing label; stand under a suspended load; exceed safe working load; use makeshift arrangements; or drag chains/ropes on the floor.

Maintenance of common elements

  • Wire rope: wear gloves to inspect; clean with a brush (no solvents — they destroy textile/synthetic cores); check the full length and the sheaves; lubricate per maker; scrap any rope with a broken strand, visible broken wires, deformation, corrosion, kinks or crushing.
  • Chain slings: thorough examination by a competent person at least every 12 months; replace bent/cracked/gouged links and deformed fittings; wear must never exceed 10% of original dimension; withdraw an overloaded sling; store on a rack (never on the floor); do not heat-treat, galvanize or pickle alloy chains; usable −40 °C to 200 °C without WLL reduction, withdraw if overheated.
  • Hooks: inspect before use; remove from service on cracks/nicks/gouges, twist >10°, latch damage, throat opening >15%, wear >10%, heat damage or unauthorized repair; grind lengthwise and replace if more than 10% of dimension is lost; never weld/heat/reshape a hook; ensure the hook (not the latch) supports the load and never side/back/point load (point loading can cut capacity by 60%).
  • Clamps: keep parts clean, wipe after every use, oil moving parts to prevent rust, store dry.
Preventive maintenance of handling equipment has three stages — inspection, repair and overhaul — to detect and correct wear before breakdown and keep production flow uninterrupted.

Seven Defects of Wire Ropes

Drawing question

A wire rope is withdrawn from service when it shows any of these seven defects (exam: "sketch and explain seven defects"). Inspect the full length — including the sections over sheaves and at the eyes — with gloves, and never drag the rope on the floor.

#DefectWhat you see / why it matters
1Abrasion / wearOuter wires worn smooth from rubbing on sheaves, drums or surfaces; reduces diameter and strength. Discard at the maker's wear limit.
2Broken wiresIndividual wires snapped (visible or protruding). Discovered by probing with a pointed tool; a rope is condemned when broken wires in a lay length exceed the limit.
3Corrosion / rustPitting or general rust, especially inside where it hides. Corroded wires break without warning; lubricate and store dry to prevent it.
4KinkA sharp, permanent bend from the rope being twisted or looped under load/tension. A kink crushes internal wires — the rope must be scrapped.
5Bird-cagingStrands bulge outward like a cage, usually from sudden release of tension or overload; the rope's lay is destroyed and it is unsafe.
6Crushing / flatteningThe rope is squashed out of round (e.g. run over, pinched in a sheave) — strength and flexibility are lost; often accompanies bird-caging.
7Heat damageDiscolouration or melted yarn/core from contact with heat, welding spatter or molten metal; the core is weakened even if the outer wires look intact.
Also scrap if: the rope has lost more than 10% of its breaking strength, its diameter is reduced beyond the limit, a strand is broken, or the identification label is missing. Lubricate per the maker's instruction and never clean with solvents (they attack synthetic/textile cores).

Hearing Conservation Program

Production industries

High noise in production industries (forging, textile, heavy machining) causes permanent hearing loss. OSHA requires a hearing conservation program whenever employee noise exposure equals or exceeds an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA) of 85 dB(A) — the exposure action value — or equivalently a 50% dose. Benefits include preserved communication, music enjoyment and warning-sound detection, early discovery of non-occupational hearing loss via annual audiograms, and lower stress, accident rates and fatigue.

Key elements

  • Noise monitoring: measure intensity and duration — area monitoring with a sound-level meter, or personal dosimetry (mic on the shoulder near the ear) for true unprotected exposure.
  • Audiometric testing: annual hearing test for all enrolled employees in a quiet booth/room; baseline at start, then annual audiograms to track change.
  • Hearing protection selection & fit testing: provide a variety of protectors with suitable attenuation; ear-plug fit-testing confirms optimal protection or flags retraining/another model.
  • Employee training & education: effects of noise, use of protectors, meaning of the hearing test, and program expectations.
  • Record keeping: document hearing history, baseline and annual audiograms, training and fit-test results.
  • Program evaluation: continually review performance via employee feedback and record review.
Hierarchy of control: reduce noise at source first (engineering and administrative controls), and use ear plugs/muffs only where exposure remains above the 85 dB(A) action level.
