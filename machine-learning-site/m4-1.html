Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction

Unsupervised learning is about finding structure in data without labels. In this module we cover two of its most important techniques: clustering — grouping similar data points together (hierarchical agglomerative clustering, k-means, and the soft-clustering Expectation-Maximisation algorithm) — and dimensionality reduction — compressing many features down to fewer while keeping the important information (Principal Component Analysis).

01

Similarity Measures

How "close" is close?

Clustering is built on a notion of similarity (or, equivalently, distance) between data points. To decide which points belong together we need a way to measure how alike two feature vectors are. Different tasks call for different measures; picking the right one is part of the job.

|x − y|1Manhattan distance

Also called L1 distance or city-block distance. For two points a = (x₁, y₁) and b = (x₂, y₂) it is

D(a,b) = |x₂ − x₁| + |y₂ − y₁|

It measures distance along a grid — like walking city blocks. It is robust to outliers because it does not square the coordinate differences, so a single large coordinate difference does not dominate.

|x − y|2Euclidean distance

The straight-line distance — the L2 norm. For a = (x₁, y₁) and b = (x₂, y₂):

D(a,b) = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]

This is the most common default. It is sensitive to large differences (because of the square) and to the scale of each feature, so features are often normalised first so that no single feature dominates.

(Σ |xᵢ − yᵢ|p)1/pMinkowski distance

A family that includes Manhattan (p=1) and Euclidean (p=2) as special cases. For general p:

Dp(a,b) = ( Σ |xᵢ − yᵢ|p )1/p

Different p values give different sensitivity to large coordinate differences. p=1 is robust; p=2 is the usual straight-line distance; larger p emphasises the largest single difference.

cosine similarity

Measures the angle between two vectors rather than their absolute distance. For vectors x and y:

cos θ = (x·y) / (‖x‖ · ‖y‖)

Two vectors pointing the same direction have cosine similarity 1 even if they are far apart in magnitude. This is useful when magnitude is not important — e.g. comparing document term-frequency vectors, where a longer document is not "more similar" just because its counts are larger.

Which to use for images? For comparing feature vectors of two images, the best choice depends on the features. Euclidean distance is a strong default for dense, normalised feature vectors (e.g. pixel intensities, CNN embeddings) because it captures overall visual similarity. Cosine similarity is preferred when the direction of the feature vector matters more than its scale (e.g. bag-of-visual-words or normalised embeddings). Manhattan distance is a robust alternative when outliers are a concern. The justification is always: "the features are X, so the distance that treats X correctly is Y."
02

Clustering — The Big Picture

Groups without labels

Clustering partitions a set of unlabelled data points into clusters — groups of points that are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. There is no "correct" answer provided; the algorithm discovers the grouping from the data itself.

Two broad families appear in this syllabus:

HACHierarchical Agglomerative Clustering

Builds a hierarchy of clusters, bottom-up. Each point starts as its own cluster; the two closest clusters are merged repeatedly until one cluster remains (or until a desired number is reached). The result can be drawn as a dendrogram — a tree showing the merge sequence and at what distance each merge happened.

"Agglomerative" = bottom-up (merge). "Divisive" = top-down (split), less common. HAC does not need the number of clusters k up front — you can cut the dendrogram at any level to get a clustering.

k-meansPartitional (k-means) Clustering

Divides the data into exactly k clusters in one level (no hierarchy). It is iterative: start with k initial centroids, assign each point to the nearest centroid, recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points, repeat until stable.

Fast and simple, but you must choose k in advance, the result depends on the initial centroids, and it tends to find spherical clusters of similar size.

03

Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering

Bottom-up, dendrogram

Agglomerative clustering starts with every point in its own cluster and merges the closest pair of clusters step by step. The key design choice is the linkage — how to measure the distance between two clusters (each of which may contain many points).

SingleSingle linkage

The distance between two clusters is the minimum distance between any point in one and any point in the other.

Strength: can find clusters of arbitrary shape — clusters can be chained together by a thin path of close points.

Weakness: sensitive to noise and outliers (a single stray point can bridge two clusters), and can produce long, straggly clusters.

CompleteComplete linkage

The distance between two clusters is the maximum distance between any point in one and any point in the other.

Strength: tends to produce compact, tight clusters — the merge distance is the worst-case distance, so clusters stay close together.

Weakness: can be sensitive to outliers; may split natural large clusters.

AverageAverage linkage

The distance between two clusters is the average of all pairwise distances between their points.

Strength: a compromise between single and complete — less sensitive to outliers than single, less rigid than complete.

Weakness: more computation than single (all pairwise distances), but generally a robust, balanced choice.

Worked example — single linkage: given the one-dimensional data (2, 5, 9, 15, 16, 18, 25, 33, 33, 45), start with each value as its own cluster. Find the smallest gap between any two values: 15 and 16 differ by 1 → merge into {15,16}. Next smallest gap: 33 and 33 differ by 0 → merge into {33,33}. Continue merging the closest clusters: 16 and 18 differ by 2 → merge {15,16} with {18} → {15,16,18}; 9 and 15 differ by 6 → merge {9} with {15,16,18} → {9,15,16,18}; 5 and 9 differ by 4 → merge {5} with {9,15,16,18}, and so on. The sequence of merges and the distances at which they happen form the dendrogram. The single-linkage distance between two clusters is the smallest distance between any member of one and any member of the other.
How to read the dendrogram: the vertical axis is the distance at which clusters merge. To get a flat clustering with a chosen number of clusters, cut the dendrogram horizontally at the appropriate height — everything joined below the cut is one cluster. Cutting higher gives fewer, larger clusters; cutting lower gives more, smaller clusters.
04

K-Means Clustering

The workhorse

K-means is the most widely used clustering algorithm. It partitions the data into exactly k clusters by iteratively refining k centroids (the representative centre of each cluster) and the assignment of points to the nearest centroid.

AlgorithmThe k-means steps

  1. Choose k initial centroids — often by picking k points that are far apart (a better start than random).
  2. Assign each point to the cluster whose centroid is nearest (by the chosen distance measure).
  3. Update each centroid to be the mean of the points now assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the assignments stop changing (convergence).
Why it converges: each iteration reduces (or keeps equal) the total within-cluster distance — the sum of distances from each point to its cluster centroid. Since this value can only decrease and is bounded below by zero, the algorithm must eventually stop.

TipChoosing initial centroids

A poor start can lead to a bad local optimum. A good heuristic: pick the first centroid randomly, then pick each next centroid as the point farthest from the already-chosen centroids. This spreads the initial centres out and usually leads to a better final clustering.

Tip from the syllabus model paper: "Try using initial cluster centers as far apart as possible" — this is exactly the far-apart initialisation heuristic.
StrengthWeakness
K-means Simple, fast, scales to large datasets, easy to implement, widely understood Must choose k in advance; result depends on initial centroids (local optimum); assumes roughly spherical, similarly-sized clusters; sensitive to outliers; needs numeric features (uses means)
HAC No need to choose k up front; produces a full hierarchy (dendrogram); flexible linkage choices; can capture non-spherical shapes (with single linkage) More expensive (O(n²) or worse); dendrogram interpretation can be subjective; single linkage sensitive to noise; complete linkage can be inflexible
Worked k-means example (k=2, far-apart start): data = (2,0), (1,2), (2,2), (3,2), (2,3), (3,3), (2,4), (3,4), (4,4), (3,5). Pick two initial centroids far apart — say (2,0) and (3,5). Assign each point to the nearest centroid (Euclidean), recompute centroids as the mean of assigned points, repeat. After convergence the two clusters settle around their final centroids. The key is: assignments and centroids change together each iteration, moving toward a stable partition.
Worked k-means example (k=3, Manhattan distance): points x₁..x₈ grouped initially as C₁={x₁,x₂,x₃}, C₂={x₄,x₅,x₆}, C₃={x₇,x₈}. Using Manhattan distance, assign each point to the nearest cluster centroid, recompute each centroid as the mean, repeat until convergence. The same algorithm works with any distance measure — here Manhattan instead of Euclidean — which changes which cluster a borderline point joins.

Expectation-Maximisation (EM) for Soft Clustering

Probabilistic groups

K-means is hard clustering — each point belongs to exactly one cluster. EM (Expectation-Maximisation) is soft (or probabilistic) clustering: each point gets a probability of belonging to each cluster, rather than a firm assignment. EM is most commonly described for Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), where the data is modelled as a mixture of several Gaussian distributions.

EM is designed for exactly the situation the syllabus names: "find a maximum likelihood setting of the parameters of a model when some of the data is missing." In clustering, the "missing data" is the cluster membership — we do not know which point came from which component, so we treat the memberships as latent (hidden) variables and estimate them alongside the model parameters.

E-stepExpectation

Given the current guess of the model parameters (the means, covariances, and mixing weights of the Gaussians), compute the probability (responsibility) that each point belongs to each cluster. This is the "expected" cluster membership, given what we currently believe about the model.

M-stepMaximisation

Given the responsibilities from the E-step, re-estimate the model parameters — update the means, covariances, and mixing weights to maximise the likelihood of the data under the current soft assignments. This is the "maximisation" of the expected complete-data log-likelihood.

The two steps repeat: E uses the current parameters to assign soft memberships; M uses those memberships to update the parameters. Each iteration increases the likelihood — EM is a hill-climbing algorithm that is guaranteed to converge to a (local) maximum of the likelihood.

Does EM converge? Yes — the likelihood increases (or stays flat) at every iteration and is bounded above, so EM converges to a stationary point. However, it converges to a locally optimal set of parameters, not necessarily global. The result depends on the initial parameters (just as k-means depends on its initial centroids). Multiple random restarts are commonly used to reduce the chance of a poor local optimum.
EM vs k-means: k-means is almost a special case of EM for Gaussian mixtures where every cluster has the same spherical (identity) covariance and the assignments are hard (each point's responsibility is 1 for one cluster, 0 for the rest). EM is the generalisation: soft assignments, full covariance matrices, and a proper probabilistic model.

Dimensionality Reduction — Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Compress without losing the gist

Many datasets have dozens or hundreds of features, and many of those features are correlated — they carry overlapping information. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a technique that finds a new set of uncorrelated features (the principal components) that are linear combinations of the original features, ordered so that the first few capture most of the variation in the data. We can then keep only the first few components and discard the rest, reducing dimensionality while losing as little information as possible.

What PCA does

PCA finds the directions (principal components) in which the data varies the most, and projects the data onto those directions. The first principal component is the direction of largest variance; the second is the direction of largest variance orthogonal to the first; and so on.

Which eigenvalue indicates the direction of largest variance? The largest eigenvalue. Each principal component has an associated eigenvalue that measures how much variance that component captures. The component with the largest eigenvalue is the first principal component — the direction along which the data is most spread out.

How to use it

(1) Standardise the features (so scale does not dominate). (2) Compute the covariance matrix of the data. (3) Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. (4) Sort components by eigenvalue, largest first. (5) Keep the top k components and project the data onto them. The kept components are your reduced-dimension representation.

Why it helps: reduces storage and computation, removes redundant/correlated features, can reduce overfitting, and lets you visualise high-dimensional data in 2 or 3 dimensions. The trade-off is some information loss — but PCA loses the least information for a given number of dimensions.
PCA in one line: rotate the data to a new coordinate system where the axes are the directions of decreasing variance, then drop the axes with the smallest variance. The new axes (principal components) are uncorrelated by construction, and are ordered by how much of the data's spread they explain. It is an unsupervised method — it does not use class labels, only the structure of the input features.

Hierarchical (Single-Linkage) — Worked Example

From the handwritten notes

Agglomerative clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters. Single linkage defines cluster distance as the minimum distance between any two points in the clusters.

Distance matrix (A, B, C, D)

A–B=10, A–C=12, A–D=20, B–C=5, B–D=3, C–D=4

Step 1

Min distance = 3 → merge B & D into {BD}.

d(A,{BD}) = min(d(A,B), d(A,D)) = min(10,20) = 10

d(C,{BD}) = min(d(C,B), d(C,D)) = min(5,4) = 4

Reduced matrix: A–{BD}=10, A–C=12, {BD}–C=4. Next merge: C & {BD} (dist 4) or A & C (dist 12).
