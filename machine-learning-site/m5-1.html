Module 5 · Classification Assessment · 7 hours

Performance Measures, Cross Validation, Ensembles, Bias–Variance, Face Detection

Building a classifier is only half the story — evaluating it honestly is the other half. This module covers how to measure whether a classifier is any good (beyond raw accuracy), how to get reliable estimates of performance (cross validation, bootstrapping), how to combine several weak classifiers into a strong one (ensembles: bagging and boosting), the fundamental bias–variance decomposition that explains why models over- and under-fit, and a concrete case study: building a face detector.

01

Classification Performance Measures

Beyond accuracy

When a classifier makes predictions, every prediction is one of four kinds, relative to the true class. These four counts form the foundation of every performance measure in binary classification.

Predicted YES (positive)Predicted NO (negative)
Actual YES (positive)TP — True PositiveFN — False Negative
Actual NO (negative)FP — False PositiveTN — True Negative

TPTrue Positive

The classifier said YES and the truth is YES. A correct positive prediction — the classifier caught a real positive case.

FPFalse Positive

The classifier said YES but the truth is NO. A "false alarm" — the classifier raised an alert on a negative case. Also called a Type I error.

FNFalse Negative

The classifier said NO but the truth is YES. A "miss" — the classifier failed to catch a real positive case. Also called a Type II error.

TNTrue Negative

The classifier said NO and the truth is NO. A correct negative prediction — correctly ignored a negative case.

MeasureFormulaMeaning
Accuracy(TP + TN) / (TP + FP + TN + FN)Fraction of all predictions that are correct. Intuitive but misleading when classes are imbalanced.
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of all the cases the classifier called positive, how many really were? Answers "when it says yes, how trustworthy is it?"
Recall (Sensitivity, True Positive Rate)TP / (TP + FN)Of all the real positive cases, how many did the classifier catch? Answers "did it find the positives?"
F-Measure (F1 score)2 · (Precision·Recall) / (Precision + Recall)The harmonic mean of precision and recall — a single number that balances both. Useful when you care about both false alarms and misses.
True Positive Rate (TPR)TP / (TP + FN)Same as recall. The fraction of positives correctly identified.
False Positive Rate (FPR)FP / (FP + TN)The fraction of negatives incorrectly flagged as positive. The "false alarm rate."
Precision vs recall — the tension: a classifier that says YES to everything has perfect recall (catches all positives) but terrible precision (most of its YESes are wrong). A classifier that says YES only when it is absolutely certain has high precision but low recall (misses many positives). The right balance depends on the cost of each error: is a false alarm worse, or is a miss worse? In medical diagnosis, a missed cancer (false negative) is usually far worse than a false alarm, so recall matters more. In spam filtering, a false positive (legitimate email marked as spam) is costly, so precision matters more.
The accuracy trap — worked example: suppose a dataset has 9700 cancer-free images and 300 cancer images (3% positive). A classifier that predicts "no cancer" for everything gets accuracy = 9700/10000 = 97% — looks great, but it completely fails to detect any cancer. Now suppose the classifier predicts cancer for 210 images, of which 120 are true cancers and 90 are false alarms, and correctly says no-cancer for the rest. Then TP=120, FP=90, TN=9600, FN=180, accuracy=(120+9600)/10000=97.2%, precision=120/210≈57%, recall=120/300=40%. Accuracy barely moved, but now we have real information: the classifier catches 40% of cancers with 57% precision. This is why accuracy alone is dangerous on imbalanced data.
02

ROC Curve & AUC

The full picture of a classifier

A classifier that outputs a score (or probability) can be thresholded at different levels — "call it positive if the score is above t." As we vary the threshold, the true positive rate and false positive rate change. The ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve plots TPR against FPR for all possible thresholds, giving a complete picture of the classifier's behaviour.

ROC space

The ROC plot has FPR on the x-axis (0 to 1) and TPR on the y-axis (0 to 1). Each point on the curve is one threshold setting. The diagonal line from (0,0) to (1,1) is the line of random guessing — a classifier that guesses at chance falls on this line.

Points in ROC space:
  • (0, 1) — perfect prediction: zero false positives, all true positives caught.
  • (1, 1) — always predict positive: catches all positives (TPR=1) but also flags everything as positive (FPR=1).
  • (1, 0) — always predict negative: catches no positives (TPR=0) and flags nothing as positive (FPR=0).
  • (0, 0) — always predict negative: misses everything, false-alarm-free.
The farther a point is toward the top-left corner (0,1), the better the classifier.

AUCArea Under the Curve

AUC summarises the ROC curve as a single number — the area under it. AUC = 1 means a perfect classifier. AUC = 0.5 means random guessing (the diagonal). AUC < 0.5 means the classifier is worse than random (and you could flip its predictions to get > 0.5).

Interpretation: AUC is the probability that the classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive example higher than a randomly chosen negative example. Higher AUC = better ranking. It is a threshold-independent measure — it evaluates the classifier's ability to order examples correctly, regardless of any single threshold.
03

The Better Classifier — Training vs Test

Generalisation matters

Two classifiers can have very different behaviour on training data versus unseen test data. The key question is always: how well does it generalise?

The rule to remember: a model that is perfect on training data is suspicious. The point of machine learning is not to fit the training set — it is to perform well on data the model has never seen. A large gap between training and test performance is the clearest sign of overfitting. A small gap and decent test performance is what we want.
04

Bootstrapping & Cross Validation

Reliable performance estimates

How do we know our performance numbers are trustworthy? A single train/test split can be lucky or unlucky. Cross validation and bootstrapping are resampling methods that give more reliable estimates by repeatedly training and testing on different subsets of the data.

k-foldCross Validation

Split the data into k equal folds. Train on k−1 folds, test on the held-out fold; repeat k times so every fold is the test set once. Average the k test scores. This uses all the data for both training and testing (just at different times) and gives a more stable estimate than a single random split.

Common choice: 10-fold CV is standard. Leave-one-out (k = n) uses a single example as the test set each time — very expensive but uses almost all data for training each time. CV reduces the variance of the performance estimate and is the default tool for model selection and evaluation.

Bootstrap

Draw n samples with replacement from the original dataset of size n to form a "bootstrap sample" (about 63% of distinct original points appear, on average). Train on the bootstrap sample, test on the points not drawn (the "out-of-bag" data). Repeat many times.

Why it helps: bootstrapping lets us estimate the distribution of a statistic (e.g. accuracy) and its variance, by simulating many possible training sets. It is especially useful when data is limited. Bootstrap and CV answer the same question — "how well will this model generalise?" — with slightly different mechanics.
When to use which: cross validation is the go-to for most model evaluation and for choosing hyperparameters (e.g. which λ for regularization, which k for k-means, which degree for polynomial regression). Bootstrapping is useful when you want confidence intervals or when the dataset is small. Both are more reliable than a single hold-out split, and both help you avoid over-fitting your evaluation — picking a model that just happens to do well on one particular test split.
05

Ensemble Methods — Bagging & Boosting

Many heads are better than one

An ensemble combines several classifiers (the "base learners") to make a single prediction, usually better than any one alone. The idea is that different models make different mistakes, and by combining them — by voting or averaging — the individual errors cancel out.

BaggingBootstrap Aggregation

Train many base learners, each on a different bootstrap sample of the data (sampled with replacement). At prediction time, average the regression outputs or take a majority vote for classification.

What it does: bagging primarily reduces variance. By averaging many models trained on different samples, the fluctuations of any one model average out. It is especially effective for high-variance, low-bias models like deep decision trees. A single tree overfits; a bag of trees (Random Forest) generalises much better.

Boosting

Train base learners sequentially, each one focusing on the examples the previous ones got wrong. Early learners are weak (slightly better than random); later ones concentrate on the hard cases. The final prediction weights the learners (often by their accuracy).

What it does: boosting primarily reduces bias (and can also reduce variance). By successively correcting errors, boosting turns many weak learners into a strong one. AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting are the classic examples. Boosting is one of the most powerful and widely used techniques in practice.
Can ensemble learning using linear classifiers learn a linearly non-separable set? Yes — this is an important point. A single linear classifier can only learn a linear boundary. But an ensemble of linear classifiers can combine several linear boundaries (vote, or weighted vote) to approximate a non-linear decision surface. Each base learner draws a straight line; the ensemble's vote creates a piecewise-linear or more complex boundary that can separate data a single line cannot. (This is closely related to how boosting and to some extent how neural networks build complex functions from simple pieces.)
Boosting and ensemble learning — the relation: boosting is a form of ensemble learning. Ensemble learning is the general idea (combine multiple models); boosting is a specific ensemble strategy (sequential, error-focused, weighted combination). Bagging is another ensemble strategy (parallel, bootstrap-based, variance-reducing). Both are ensemble methods; boosting is the one that builds strength by correcting mistakes step by step.
06

Bias–Variance Decomposition

Why models fail

The expected error of a learned model on new data can be decomposed into three parts: bias, variance, and irreducible error (noise in the data itself, which no model can eliminate). This decomposition is the theoretical foundation for understanding over- and underfitting.

TermWhat it meansHigh bias looks likeHigh variance looks like
BiasError from the model being too simple / wrong assumptions — the gap between what the model can represent and the true function. Underfitting: high error on both training and test data; the model cannot capture the pattern even on data it has seen. —
VarianceError from the model being too sensitive to the particular training sample — small changes in training data produce very different models. — Overfitting: low training error, high test error; the model's predictions swing wildly with different training data.
Irreducible errorNoise inherent in the data — variability that no model can explain (measurement noise, unobserved variables). Cannot be reduced by any model; it is a property of the problem, not of the learning algorithm.
The trade-off: as a model becomes more complex, bias tends to decrease (it can represent more functions) but variance tends to increase (it fits the noise in the particular training set). The best model sits at the "sweet spot" where the sum of bias² and variance is minimised — enough complexity to capture the truth, not so much that it memorises noise. This is the bias–variance trade-off. It is the theoretical reason that regularisation (Ridge, Lasso), ensembles, and cross validation all work: they all shift this balance toward better generalisation.
Why it matters: the Bayes classifier is the accuracy ceiling — no method can beat it on average. Practical models (incl. ensembles) are trying to approximate this optimum from data.
