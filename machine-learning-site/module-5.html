Classification Performance Measures

Beyond accuracy

When a classifier makes predictions, every prediction is one of four kinds, relative to the true class. These four counts form the foundation of every performance measure in binary classification.

Predicted YES (positive)Predicted NO (negative)
Actual YES (positive)TP — True PositiveFN — False Negative
Actual NO (negative)FP — False PositiveTN — True Negative

TPTrue Positive

The classifier said YES and the truth is YES. A correct positive prediction — the classifier caught a real positive case.

FPFalse Positive

The classifier said YES but the truth is NO. A "false alarm" — the classifier raised an alert on a negative case. Also called a Type I error.

FNFalse Negative

The classifier said NO but the truth is YES. A "miss" — the classifier failed to catch a real positive case. Also called a Type II error.

TNTrue Negative

The classifier said NO and the truth is NO. A correct negative prediction — correctly ignored a negative case.

MeasureFormulaMeaning
Accuracy(TP + TN) / (TP + FP + TN + FN)Fraction of all predictions that are correct. Intuitive but misleading when classes are imbalanced.
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of all the cases the classifier called positive, how many really were? Answers "when it says yes, how trustworthy is it?"
Recall (Sensitivity, True Positive Rate)TP / (TP + FN)Of all the real positive cases, how many did the classifier catch? Answers "did it find the positives?"
F-Measure (F1 score)2 · (Precision·Recall) / (Precision + Recall)The harmonic mean of precision and recall — a single number that balances both. Useful when you care about both false alarms and misses.
True Positive Rate (TPR)TP / (TP + FN)Same as recall. The fraction of positives correctly identified.
False Positive Rate (FPR)FP / (FP + TN)The fraction of negatives incorrectly flagged as positive. The "false alarm rate."
Precision vs recall — the tension: a classifier that says YES to everything has perfect recall (catches all positives) but terrible precision (most of its YESes are wrong). A classifier that says YES only when it is absolutely certain has high precision but low recall (misses many positives). The right balance depends on the cost of each error: is a false alarm worse, or is a miss worse? In medical diagnosis, a missed cancer (false negative) is usually far worse than a false alarm, so recall matters more. In spam filtering, a false positive (legitimate email marked as spam) is costly, so precision matters more.
The accuracy trap — worked example: suppose a dataset has 9700 cancer-free images and 300 cancer images (3% positive). A classifier that predicts "no cancer" for everything gets accuracy = 9700/10000 = 97% — looks great, but it completely fails to detect any cancer. Now suppose the classifier predicts cancer for 210 images, of which 120 are true cancers and 90 are false alarms, and correctly says no-cancer for the rest. Then TP=120, FP=90, TN=9600, FN=180, accuracy=(120+9600)/10000=97.2%, precision=120/210≈57%, recall=120/300=40%. Accuracy barely moved, but now we have real information: the classifier catches 40% of cancers with 57% precision. This is why accuracy alone is dangerous on imbalanced data.
02

ROC Curve & AUC

The full picture of a classifier

A classifier that outputs a score (or probability) can be thresholded at different levels — "call it positive if the score is above t." As we vary the threshold, the true positive rate and false positive rate change. The ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve plots TPR against FPR for all possible thresholds, giving a complete picture of the classifier's behaviour.

ROC space

The ROC plot has FPR on the x-axis (0 to 1) and TPR on the y-axis (0 to 1). Each point on the curve is one threshold setting. The diagonal line from (0,0) to (1,1) is the line of random guessing — a classifier that guesses at chance falls on this line.

Points in ROC space:
  • (0, 1) — perfect prediction: zero false positives, all true positives caught.
  • (1, 1) — always predict positive: catches all positives (TPR=1) but also flags everything as positive (FPR=1).
  • (1, 0) — always predict negative: catches no positives (TPR=0) and flags nothing as positive (FPR=0).
  • (0, 0) — always predict negative: misses everything, false-alarm-free.
The farther a point is toward the top-left corner (0,1), the better the classifier.

AUCArea Under the Curve

AUC summarises the ROC curve as a single number — the area under it. AUC = 1 means a perfect classifier. AUC = 0.5 means random guessing (the diagonal). AUC < 0.5 means the classifier is worse than random (and you could flip its predictions to get > 0.5).

Interpretation: AUC is the probability that the classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive example higher than a randomly chosen negative example. Higher AUC = better ranking. It is a threshold-independent measure — it evaluates the classifier's ability to order examples correctly, regardless of any single threshold.
03

The Better Classifier — Training vs Test

Generalisation matters

Two classifiers can have very different behaviour on training data versus unseen test data. The key question is always: how well does it generalise?

The rule to remember: a model that is perfect on training data is suspicious. The point of machine learning is not to fit the training set — it is to perform well on data the model has never seen. A large gap between training and test performance is the clearest sign of overfitting. A small gap and decent test performance is what we want.
04

Bootstrapping & Cross Validation

Reliable performance estimates

How do we know our performance numbers are trustworthy? A single train/test split can be lucky or unlucky. Cross validation and bootstrapping are resampling methods that give more reliable estimates by repeatedly training and testing on different subsets of the data.

k-foldCross Validation

Split the data into k equal folds. Train on k−1 folds, test on the held-out fold; repeat k times so every fold is the test set once. Average the k test scores. This uses all the data for both training and testing (just at different times) and gives a more stable estimate than a single random split.

Common choice: 10-fold CV is standard. Leave-one-out (k = n) uses a single example as the test set each time — very expensive but uses almost all data for training each time. CV reduces the variance of the performance estimate and is the default tool for model selection and evaluation.

Bootstrap

Draw n samples with replacement from the original dataset of size n to form a "bootstrap sample" (about 63% of distinct original points appear, on average). Train on the bootstrap sample, test on the points not drawn (the "out-of-bag" data). Repeat many times.

Why it helps: bootstrapping lets us estimate the distribution of a statistic (e.g. accuracy) and its variance, by simulating many possible training sets. It is especially useful when data is limited. Bootstrap and CV answer the same question — "how well will this model generalise?" — with slightly different mechanics.
When to use which: cross validation is the go-to for most model evaluation and for choosing hyperparameters (e.g. which λ for regularization, which k for k-means, which degree for polynomial regression). Bootstrapping is useful when you want confidence intervals or when the dataset is small. Both are more reliable than a single hold-out split, and both help you avoid over-fitting your evaluation — picking a model that just happens to do well on one particular test split.

Ensemble Methods — Bagging & Boosting

Many heads are better than one

An ensemble combines several classifiers (the "base learners") to make a single prediction, usually better than any one alone. The idea is that different models make different mistakes, and by combining them — by voting or averaging — the individual errors cancel out.

BaggingBootstrap Aggregation

Train many base learners, each on a different bootstrap sample of the data (sampled with replacement). At prediction time, average the regression outputs or take a majority vote for classification.

What it does: bagging primarily reduces variance. By averaging many models trained on different samples, the fluctuations of any one model average out. It is especially effective for high-variance, low-bias models like deep decision trees. A single tree overfits; a bag of trees (Random Forest) generalises much better.

Boosting

Train base learners sequentially, each one focusing on the examples the previous ones got wrong. Early learners are weak (slightly better than random); later ones concentrate on the hard cases. The final prediction weights the learners (often by their accuracy).

What it does: boosting primarily reduces bias (and can also reduce variance). By successively correcting errors, boosting turns many weak learners into a strong one. AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting are the classic examples. Boosting is one of the most powerful and widely used techniques in practice.
Can ensemble learning using linear classifiers learn a linearly non-separable set? Yes — this is an important point. A single linear classifier can only learn a linear boundary. But an ensemble of linear classifiers can combine several linear boundaries (vote, or weighted vote) to approximate a non-linear decision surface. Each base learner draws a straight line; the ensemble's vote creates a piecewise-linear or more complex boundary that can separate data a single line cannot. (This is closely related to how boosting and to some extent how neural networks build complex functions from simple pieces.)
Boosting and ensemble learning — the relation: boosting is a form of ensemble learning. Ensemble learning is the general idea (combine multiple models); boosting is a specific ensemble strategy (sequential, error-focused, weighted combination). Bagging is another ensemble strategy (parallel, bootstrap-based, variance-reducing). Both are ensemble methods; boosting is the one that builds strength by correcting mistakes step by step.

Bias–Variance Decomposition

Why models fail

The expected error of a learned model on new data can be decomposed into three parts: bias, variance, and irreducible error (noise in the data itself, which no model can eliminate). This decomposition is the theoretical foundation for understanding over- and underfitting.

TermWhat it meansHigh bias looks likeHigh variance looks like
BiasError from the model being too simple / wrong assumptions — the gap between what the model can represent and the true function. Underfitting: high error on both training and test data; the model cannot capture the pattern even on data it has seen. —
VarianceError from the model being too sensitive to the particular training sample — small changes in training data produce very different models. — Overfitting: low training error, high test error; the model's predictions swing wildly with different training data.
Irreducible errorNoise inherent in the data — variability that no model can explain (measurement noise, unobserved variables). Cannot be reduced by any model; it is a property of the problem, not of the learning algorithm.
The trade-off: as a model becomes more complex, bias tends to decrease (it can represent more functions) but variance tends to increase (it fits the noise in the particular training set). The best model sits at the "sweet spot" where the sum of bias² and variance is minimised — enough complexity to capture the truth, not so much that it memorises noise. This is the bias–variance trade-off. It is the theoretical reason that regularisation (Ridge, Lasso), ensembles, and cross validation all work: they all shift this balance toward better generalisation.
Why it matters: the Bayes classifier is the accuracy ceiling — no method can beat it on average. Practical models (incl. ensembles) are trying to approximate this optimum from data.
07

Bias–Variance Decomposition

Theory

The expected test error splits into three parts:

\[\text{Error} = \text{Bias}^{2} + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible noise}\]
  • Bias — error from a too-simple model (underfitting); it consistently misses the pattern.
  • Variance — error from a too-sensitive model (overfitting); it changes a lot with small data changes.
The trade-off: as model complexity rises, bias falls but variance grows. The sweet spot minimizes their sum. Regularization and more data both help push the frontier.

Cross-Validation

Honest estimate

k-fold CV: split data into k equal parts; train on k−1, test on the held-out part; repeat k times and average the scores. Common choices: k = 5 or 10.

\[\text{CV score}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{error on fold }j\]

This uses all data for both train and test (just never at the same time), giving a stable estimate of how the model will perform on unseen data. Bootstrapping is the cousin that resamples with replacement to build confidence intervals.

Why not just use the training accuracy? a model can score 100% on data it already saw and fail on new data. CV measures generalization, not memorization.

The Confusion Matrix — Every Metric Computed from One Example

Evaluation

For a binary classifier, all evaluation metrics derive from four counts. The convention is that the positive class is the one you care about detecting (cancer, fraud, face). A true positive (TP) is a correct alert; a false positive (FP) is a false alarm (Type I error); a false negative (FN) is a miss (Type II error); a true negative (TN) is a correct rejection.

\[\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N},\quad \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad \text{Recall (TPR)}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\]
\[F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN},\quad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},\quad \text{Specificity}=1-\text{FPR}=\frac{TN}{FP+TN}\]

Worked example — imbalanced screening

A screening test on N = 1000 people yields TP = 50, FN = 10, FP = 30, TN = 910. (There are 60 true positives and 940 negatives.) Then:

\[\text{Accuracy}=\frac{50+910}{1000}=0.960,\qquad \text{Precision}=\frac{50}{50+30}=0.625,\qquad \text{Recall}=\frac{50}{50+10}=0.833\]
\[F_1=\frac{2(0.625)(0.833)}{0.625+0.833}=0.714,\qquad \text{FPR}=\frac{30}{30+910}=0.0319,\qquad \text{Specificity}=0.968\]

The 96% accuracy looks excellent, but the classifier misses one in six diseased patients. If we instead predicted "negative" for everybody, accuracy would be 940/1000 = 94% with recall zero — only two points below, yet useless. This is why accuracy is unsuitable for imbalanced data.

Precision–recall tension: lowering the decision threshold flags more cases, raising recall and lowering precision; raising it does the reverse. Choose the threshold from the application: for cancer screening a false negative is far costlier, so accept low precision; for spam filtering a false positive (a lost business email) is costlier, so favour precision. F1 is the harmonic mean because it punishes extreme imbalance between the two — the arithmetic mean would hide a model with precision 1 and recall 0.01.
Multiclass extension: for K classes compute a confusion matrix with K² entries. Micro-averaging pools counts over all classes (equivalent to accuracy when every example has one label); macro-averaging computes the metric per class and takes the unweighted mean, giving each class equal say — macro is preferred when classes are imbalanced.

ROC Curves and AUC — Construction, Trapezoid Arithmetic and Interpretation

Threshold-free evaluation

A classifier producing a score s(x) can be thresholded at any t. Each threshold yields one (FPR, TPR) pair; plotting all of them gives the ROC curve. The diagonal from (0,0) to (1,1) is random guessing. The perfect classifier passes through the top-left corner (0,1).

Worked AUC from five operating points

Suppose sweeping the threshold gives these (FPR, TPR) points, already sorted by FPR: (0, 0), (0.1, 0.5), (0.3, 0.7), (0.6, 0.9), (1, 1). Approximate the area under the curve by trapezoids:

segment 1: (0.1-0.0) * (0.0+0.5)/2 = 0.1 * 0.25 = 0.025
segment 2: (0.3-0.1) * (0.5+0.7)/2 = 0.2 * 0.60 = 0.120
segment 3: (0.6-0.3) * (0.7+0.9)/2 = 0.3 * 0.80 = 0.240
segment 4: (1.0-0.6) * (0.9+1.0)/2 = 0.4 * 0.95 = 0.380
AUC = 0.025 + 0.120 + 0.240 + 0.380 = 0.765
\[\text{AUC}=P\bigl(s(\text{random positive})>s(\text{random negative})\bigr)\]

This probabilistic interpretation explains why AUC is threshold-independent: it measures how well the classifier ranks cases. AUC = 0.5 is chance, 0.7–0.8 is considered acceptable, above 0.9 excellent. A classifier with AUC below 0.5 can simply have its decisions inverted to obtain above 0.5.

ROC vs precision–recall curves: PR curves plot precision against recall. When negatives vastly outnumber positives, ROC can look optimistic because FPR changes slowly (the denominator FP + TN is huge); PR curves expose the poor precision. Use PR/AUC-PR for heavily imbalanced problems, ROC/AUC for balanced ones.
Choosing an operating point: Youden's J = TPR + Specificity − 1 = TPR − FPR picks the point farthest above the diagonal; the closest-to-(0,1) point minimises √((1−TPR)² + FPR²). In practice choose the threshold that satisfies a domain constraint, e.g. "FPR ≤ 5%", then maximise TPR subject to it — exactly the false-positive-budget language used in the face-detection case study.

Honest Evaluation — Train/Validation/Test, k-Fold CV and Bootstrap Arithmetic

Resampling

A single hold-out split is a random estimate: a lucky split inflates performance. Cross-validation averages over splits to reduce that variance. The golden rule: the test set is touched once, after all model and hyperparameter choices are made; the validation set is used for those choices; the training set is used to fit parameters. Nesting these loops avoids leaking information.

\[\widehat{\mathrm{CV}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{err}\bigl(h^{(-j)},D_j\bigr),\qquad h^{(-j)}=\text{model trained without fold }j\]

Worked k-fold calculation

Five-fold CV on a dataset yields fold errors 0.12, 0.10, 0.14, 0.11, 0.13. Mean = 0.60/5 = 0.12; the sample standard deviation is √[(0² + 0.02² + 0.02² + 0.01² + 0.01²)/4] = √(0.001/4) = 0.0158. Report the mean as the performance estimate and the standard deviation (or a standard error = s/√k = 0.0071) as its uncertainty. k = 5 or 10 is standard; leave-one-out (k = n) has low bias but high computational cost and high variance of the estimate because the training sets differ by only one example.

Bootstrap — where 63.2% comes from

Drawing n samples with replacement from a set of size n, the probability a particular original point is never chosen is (1 − 1/n)n → e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368 as n grows. Hence about 63.2% of distinct points appear in a bootstrap sample and 36.8% are out-of-bag. For n = 10 the in-bag fraction is 1 − 0.9¹⁰ = 0.651, already close to the limit. The out-of-bag points provide a free test set — the basis of bagging's out-of-bag error estimate.

Data leakage to watch for: scaling or feature selection must be fitted on the training fold only, then applied to the validation fold; fitting them on all data leaks information and inflates the CV score. The same applies to hyperparameter tuning: choosing λ using the test set turns it into a training set. Nested CV (outer loop for evaluation, inner loop for tuning) is the rigorous fix.

Ensembles — Why Averaging Works, and the AdaBoost Update Arithmetic

Combining models

Ensembles combine base learners to reduce error. Suppose m models each have variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ. The variance of their average is

\[\operatorname{Var}\Bigl(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_i\Bigr)=\rho\sigma^{2}+\frac{1-\rho}{m}\sigma^{2}\]

With independent models (ρ = 0) the variance falls to σ²/m — averaging m models cuts variance by a factor of m. This is the mathematical core of bagging: train many models on bootstrap samples so their errors are decorrelated, then average (regression) or majority-vote (classification). Random Forests add random feature subsampling at each split to decorrelate the trees further.

Boosting works differently: it trains weak learners sequentially, reweighting the data so each new learner focuses on previously misclassified examples. In AdaBoost, for base learner t with weighted error εₜ,

\[\alpha_t=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t},\qquad w_i\leftarrow w_i\exp\bigl(-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)\bigr),\qquad H(x)=\operatorname{sign}\Bigl(\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x)\Bigr)\]

Worked: round 1 has weighted error ε₁ = 0.2, so α₁ = 0.5 ln(0.8/0.2) = 0.5 ln 4 = 0.693. Round 2 has ε₂ = 0.3, so α₂ = 0.5 ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.5 ln(2.333) = 0.424. Misclassified points (yᵢh₁(xᵢ) = −1) have their weights multiplied by e+0.693 = 2.0, while correct points are multiplied by e−0.693 = 0.5 — the next learner sees twice as much emphasis on the hard cases. In the final vote the first classifier has 1.63 times the say of the second. Boosting primarily reduces bias (a sequence of weak learners approximates a complex function); bagging primarily reduces variance.

Can an ensemble of linear classifiers separate non-linear data? Yes. Each base learner draws a straight boundary; a weighted vote of several such boundaries produces a piecewise-linear region, which can be non-convex and non-linearly separable as a whole. This is the same constructive principle as a neural network's hidden layer and as kernel SVMs: combine simple pieces to build a complex decision surface.
MethodTrainingCombinationMain effectExample
BaggingParallel bootstrap samplesAverage / majority voteVariance ↓Random Forest
BoostingSequential, reweightedWeighted voteBias ↓ (sometimes variance too)AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost
StackingParallel diverse modelsLearned meta-modelBoth, if meta-learner is trained honestlyBlended regressors/classifiers
Why ensembles can still fail: if base models make the same mistakes (ρ ≈ 1), averaging buys almost nothing — diversity is essential. Boosting can overfit noisy labels because it keeps chasing outliers; bagging with deep trees is more robust. Always use cross-validation to choose the number of boosting rounds (early stopping).

Bias–Variance Decomposition — The Algebra and Two Contrasting Models

Theory

For a fixed test input x, let the true value be f(x) = E[y | x] and the observed target be y = f(x) + ε with E[ε] = 0, Var(ε) = σ². For a model ŷ trained on a random sample, the expected squared error decomposes as

\[\mathbb E\bigl[(y-\hat y)^2\bigr]=\underbrace{\bigl(f(x)-\mathbb E[\hat y]\bigr)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\bigl[(\hat y-\mathbb E[\hat y])^2\bigr]}_{\text{Variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}\]

To derive it, write y − ŷ = (f − E[ŷ]) + (E[ŷ] − ŷ) − ε; squaring gives the three squared terms plus cross terms. Taking expectations kills the cross terms: E[E[ŷ] − ŷ] = 0 and ε is independent of ŷ with mean zero. What remains is exactly the expression above.

Two contrasting predictors

Suppose the true value at x is f(x) = 5 and noise variance is σ² = 0.5. Model A is a very flexible fit whose predictions on five repeated training samples are 4, 5, 6, 5, 5 — mean 5, bias 0, variance [(−1)²+0²+1²+0²+0²]/5 = 0.4, expected error ≈ 0 + 0.4 + 0.5 = 0.9. Model B is a rigid predictor that always answers 4.3 — bias² = (5 − 4.3)² = 0.49, variance 0, expected error ≈ 0.49 + 0 + 0.5 = 0.99. In this case the flexible model wins; if its spread were larger (predictions 2, 8, 5, 4, 6 → variance 4.4) the rigid one would win. The optimum sits where the sum of the two controllable terms is smallest.

Diagnostic → remedy table: high training error and high validation error → high bias → add features, increase model complexity, reduce regularisation, train longer. Low training error and much higher validation error → high variance → gather more data, regularise, simplify, use bagging, apply early stopping. Look at the gap and the level separately — this is the single most useful exam diagnostic.
Consistency with earlier modules: Ridge/Lasso (Module 2) raise bias to cut variance; C and γ in SVMs (Module 3) do the same; tree pruning and depth limits (Module 2) control variance; ensembling (this module) attacks whichever term dominates. EM's covariance constraints and PCA's component count (Module 4) are further examples. The bias–variance decomposition is the unifying language of the entire course.
Exam caution: bias² + variance + noise is the decomposition of expected squared error for regression. For classification the algebra is messier, but the intuition transfers and examiners accept the qualitative trade-off. Also distinguish it from the bias of an estimator in Module 1 (E[θ̂] − θ): related ideas, different objects.

Case Study — Face Detection with a Cascaded Boosted Classifier

Course synthesis

The face-detection task ties every module together. A sliding window scans an image at several scales; for each window we decide "face" or "not face". Nearly all windows are background, so the class distribution is severely imbalanced and the false-positive budget dominates the design.

1) Features: Haar-like rectangular sums computed with an integral image
   (a 24x24 window yields > 160,000 rectangle features; select a small subset).
2) Learning: AdaBoost selects the best weak "stump" each round and combines
   them into a strong classifier (Module 5 ensembles + Module 2 decisions).
3) Cascade: arrange stages from simple to complex; a window rejected by a
   stage is discarded immediately; only survivors reach later stages.
4) Evaluation: choose per-stage thresholds so the whole cascade meets a
   target detection rate and FPR, measured with ROC/AUC and k-fold CV (M5).
5) Scan: run the cascade over all positions and scales, merge overlapping
   detections.

Cascade arithmetic

Suppose each of 5 stages rejects 50% of non-face windows and passes 95% of real faces. The overall detection rate is 0.95⁵ = 0.774 (77.4% of faces survive) and the overall false-positive rate is 0.5⁵ = 0.0313 (about 3% of background windows survive). Early stages are cheap and reject most of the background, which is why the cascade is fast even with many features. Adding a sixth stage would push FPR to 1.56% but lower detection to 73.5% — a direct illustration of the precision/recall trade-off quantified in the first section.

Integral image trick: building a summed-area table lets the sum of pixels in any axis-aligned rectangle be computed with four array lookups, independent of the rectangle's size. This is what makes thousands of Haar features evaluable in real time — a reminder that feature engineering and algorithmic engineering matter as much as the learning algorithm.
Module map of this case study: features and normalisation (Module 4 distance/scaling ideas), supervised label generation (Module 1 supervised paradigm), weak learners as decision stumps (Module 2 trees), boosting (Module 5 ensembles), false-positive-budget thresholding (Module 5 ROC), and honest performance estimates (Module 5 CV). In an exam, use this case study as a concrete example whenever asked to "tie the course together".
Final revision checklist for Module 5: (1) write the confusion matrix and all six formulas from memory; (2) explain why accuracy fails under imbalance with the 97%-accuracy example; (3) sketch an ROC curve and interpret an AUC value; (4) state the k-fold procedure and the 63.2% bootstrap result; (5) contrast bagging (variance) and boosting (bias) with the α formula; (6) derive bias² + variance + noise; (7) quote the cascade numbers 0.95⁵ and 0.5⁵. Is the Difference Real? — Confidence Intervals and Paired Comparison of Classifiers

Statistical evaluation

A CV score is itself a random quantity. Reporting "classifier A scored 0.86 and B scored 0.84" without an uncertainty invites over-claiming. With k fold scores e₁ … eₖ, estimate the mean error ē and its standard error s/√k, then give a 95% interval ē ± t0.975,k−1·s/√k (t ≈ 2.57 for k = 5, 2.23 for k = 10). For the five fold errors 0.12, 0.10, 0.14, 0.11, 0.13 the mean is 0.12, s = 0.0158, standard error = 0.0071, and the interval is 0.12 ± 2.57·0.0071 = [0.102, 0.138].

To compare two classifiers on the same folds, compute the per-fold difference dⱼ = eⱼA − eⱼB and apply a paired t-test:

\[t=\frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt{k}},\qquad \bar d=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_j\]

with k − 1 degrees of freedom. Pairing removes the variability caused by the folds themselves, making the test far more sensitive than comparing two unpaired means. When folds are small or the difference is not approximately normal, use the 5×2 cross-validation variant or McNemar's test on the single held-out set. McNemar uses the counts b and c of examples that only one of the two classifiers got right and the statistic (|b − c| − 1)²/(b + c), compared with χ²₁ (critical value 3.84 at 5%).

Worked McNemar: classifier A alone is correct on b = 20 cases that B misses; B alone is correct on c = 5 cases that A misses. Then (|20 − 5| − 1)²/(20 + 5) = (14)²/25 = 196/25 = 7.84 > 3.84, so the difference is statistically significant at 5% — A is genuinely better on this test set, not just luckily.
Multiple comparisons: testing twenty hyperparameter settings at the 5% level yields roughly one false positive by chance. Apply a Bonferroni correction (use α/m as the significance level) or report the whole CV curve with error bars instead of declaring a single winner. This is the same honesty principle as never tuning on the test set.

Handling Imbalanced Data — Class Weights, Resampling and Threshold Choice

Practical classification

When positives are rare (fraud, cancer, faces), a classifier optimised for overall accuracy will ignore them. There are four complementary responses. Class weights modify the loss so a missed positive costs w times more — set w = N/(2·Nminority) so the weighted class totals are equal. Oversampling duplicates minority examples; undersampling discards majority examples; SMOTE synthesises new minority points by interpolating between nearest neighbours, avoiding pure duplication. Threshold moving keeps the trained model but lowers the decision threshold until recall reaches the required level.

\[J_{\mathrm{weighted}}=-\sum_i\bigl[w_1 r_i\ln \hat y_i+w_0(1-r_i)\ln(1-\hat y_i)\bigr],\qquad w_c=\frac{N}{2N_c}\]

Worked: in the 1000-person screening example there are 60 positives and 940 negatives. w₁ = 1000/(2·60) = 8.33 and w₀ = 1000/(2·940) = 0.532. A false negative now costs about 15.7 times as much as a false positive in the weighted loss, pushing the model toward higher recall. Note that weighting changes the effective operating point; report unweighted precision/recall on a natural test distribution to communicate the real-world performance.

Critical warning: resampling must be performed inside each training fold. Duplicating or synthesising minority points before splitting leaks near-identical examples into the test fold and produces wildly optimistic scores. Similarly, a synthetic minority point created from a validation example is contamination. The evaluation set must always reflect the natural class distribution.
Which metric to optimise? If the cost matrix is known, choose the threshold minimising expected cost = CFN·FN + CFP·FP. Otherwise optimise Fβ = (1 + β²)PR/(β²P + R), which weights recall β times as heavily as precision (β = 2 for medical screening), or use AUC-PR for ranking under heavy imbalance. Accuracy should never be the sole reported metric on an imbalanced problem.
Module 5 in one paragraph: define the confusion matrix; choose a metric matched to the error costs and class balance; plot ROC/PR to understand threshold trade-offs; estimate performance with cross-validation or bootstrap and report uncertainty; control complexity to manage bias and variance; combine models with bagging or boosting when a single learner is unstable; and, in deployment, monitor for drift because a validated model is only valid for the distribution it was validated on.

Learning Curves and the Model-Selection Workflow in Practice

Diagnosing generalisation

A learning curve plots training and validation error against the number of training examples (or, alternatively, against training iterations). Its shape tells you which action will help, and this is one of the most reliable exam questions because the answer can be reasoned out rather than memorised.

Curve shapeDiagnosisHelpful actionsUseless actions
Both errors high, small gapHigh bias / underfittingAdd features, raise complexity, reduce regularisation, train longerMore data, bagging
Training low, validation high, gap shrinking with more dataHigh variance / overfittingMore data, regularisation, bagging, simplifyMore features of the same kind
Both errors low, tiny gapGood fitCheck test set once; consider deployment driftFurther tuning on the same data
Training error rises late while validation worsensOvertrainingEarly stopping, dropout, weight decayIncreasing epochs

Worked reading of a curve

At 100 training examples a model scores 8% training error and 26% validation error; at 500 examples it scores 14% training and 18% validation. The gap has closed from 18 points to 4 while the validation error dropped from 26% to 18%. This is a high-variance learner that benefits from data; collecting more examples is the highest-value next step. If instead the numbers were 22%/24% at 100 and 21%/23% at 500, the gap is small but both errors are high — more data will not help; the model is too simple and needs richer features or lower regularisation.

Complete model-selection workflow: (1) reserve a test set and never touch it during tuning; (2) choose a metric and an operating threshold from the application's error costs; (3) run nested k-fold CV over model families and hyperparameters; (4) read the learning curves to decide whether to invest in data or in features; (5) refit the selected configuration on all training data; (6) evaluate once on the test set and report the metric with a confidence interval; (7) monitor performance after deployment for covariate or concept drift. Each step corresponds to a distinct marking point in a long-answer question.
