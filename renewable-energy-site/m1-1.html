Classification of Energy Resources

Before any renewable technology makes sense, you need the taxonomy. This page covers every way energy resources are classified — by usability, tradition, renewability, commercial use, and origin — plus the advantages and limitations of conventional vs non-conventional sources.

Classification Conventional Non-conventional Comparison
01

Classification at a Glance

Five axes

Energy resources are classified along five independent axes. A single source lands in a box on every axis — e.g. coal is primary + conventional + non-renewable + commercial + fossil.

BasisCategories
1. UsabilityPrimary → Intermediate → Secondary
2. Traditional useConventional (fossil, nuclear, hydro) / Non-conventional (solar, wind, biomass)
3. Term availabilityNon-renewable (finite) / Renewable (replenished by nature)
4. Commercial applicationCommercial (electricity, petrol, diesel) / Non-commercial (wood, dung, crop residue)
5. OriginFossil, Nuclear, Hydro, Solar, Wind, Biomass, Geothermal, Ocean
02

By Usability (Primary → Secondary)

Energy chain

PPrimary

Resources available in nature in raw form — coal, oil, gas, uranium, hydro. "Raw energy resources."

IIntermediate

Obtained from primary by one or more transformation steps; used as a "vehicle of energy" (e.g. refined fuels).

SSecondary

The final usable form supplied to the consumer: electrical energy, thermal (steam/hot water), chemical (hydrogen, refined fuels).

Chain example: coal (primary) → electricity (secondary); uranium (primary) → steam → electricity (secondary).
03

By Renewability

Finite vs infinite
TypeDefinitionExamples
Non-renewableFinite; not replenished after consumptionCoal, oil, gas, uranium
RenewableRenewed by nature repeatedly; supply unaffected by rate of useSolar, wind, biomass, ocean (thermal/tidal/wave), geothermal, hydro
Key point: renewable does not mean unlimited in power — it means the source is not depleted by extraction. Intermittency (sun, wind) is a separate issue.
04

Conventional Energy Sources

Fossil · Nuclear · Hydro

FFossil fuels

Coal, petroleum, natural gas. Formed by decomposition of buried plant/animal remains over geologic time. Non-renewable, limited reserves, pollute.

NNuclear

Heavy unstable atoms (U-235, Th-239) release heat by fission. 1 kg U-235 ≈ heat of 4500 t coal (or 220 t oil). Steam drives turbo-generators.

HHydro

Water falls under gravity → potential energy → mechanical (hydraulic turbines) → electricity. Cheap where water is abundant; low operating cost (no fuel).

Fossil fuel detail

Coal

Complex C/H/O compound (+N, S). Backbone of energy sector (industry, transport, power). Major mines: Jharia, Bokaro (Bihar), Raniganj (WB).

Petroleum

Dark viscous crude trapped in rock. Refined by fractional distillation (~400 °C): petroleum gas (LPG, <40 °C), petrol (40–170 °C), kerosene (170–250 °C), diesel (250–350 °C), fuel oil (350–400 °C), residue (lube/wax/asphalt).

Natural gas

~95% methane (+ethane, propane). Found with/above oil. Used as domestic/industrial fuel; CNG substitutes petrol in vehicles. India: Tripura, Jaisalmer, Bombay High, Krishna–Godavari.

Nuclear — limitations & Indian plants

Limitations

  • High capital cost of nuclear plants
  • Limited, expensive-to-recover raw material (U reserves small)
  • Radioactive waste disposal difficulty
  • Shortage of trained personnel

Indian plants

  • Tarapur (Maharashtra)
  • Rana Pratap Sagar (Rajasthan)
  • Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu)
  • Narora (Uttar Pradesh)
  • ~3% of India's energy from nuclear

Hydro — development problems

  • Long lead time: ~6–10 years for planning, investigation, construction.
  • High capital investment; some components imported.
  • Village relocation, compensation, resettlement, environmental impact.
05

Non-Conventional (Renewable) Sources

The alternatives

SSolar

From the Sun; intermittent, eco-friendly, non-polluting. Earth intercepts ≈1.8×10¹¹ MW. 99% of radiation is 0.2–4 µm wavelength. Used as heat or direct PV.

WWind

Kinetic energy of air masses. Caused by atmospheric heating (convection) + Earth's rotation. ~1.6×10⁷ MW potential. Uses: agriculture, rural/municipal, pumping, large-scale generation.

BBioenergy

From organic matter. Biogas = anaerobic decomposition of dung/waste → CH₄-rich gas. Biomass = energy from plants/animals, often transformed chemically/biologically first.

OOcean

71% of Earth's surface. Tidal (gravitational), wave (wind transfer), OTEC (thermal gradient). See Module 2.

GGeothermal

Heat from Earth's interior. Used directly or to raise steam for generation.

HHydro (renewable)

Large-hydro is conventional by tradition but renewable by nature — note the overlap across axes.

07

Advantages & Limitations — Both Sides

Balanced view

Conventional — Advantages

  • Cheaper than non-conventional at present
  • Easy, convenient storage → assured availability
  • Universally available conversion tech; very convenient to use

Conventional — Limitations

  • Pollutants (CO, CO₂, particulates, heat) → health/environment
  • Coal is valuable petrochem feedstock — conserve for future
  • Non-renewable → will finish; causes degradation

Non-conventional — Advantages

  • Non-polluting, renewable, eco-friendly
  • Low running cost; cheap/clean; job creation
  • Stabilizes global energy prices; can be stored in original form

Non-conventional — Limitations

  • High harnessing cost; hard to transport
  • Uncertain availability; weather-dependent/unreliable
  • Low efficiency; biogas → GHG; tidal hard to harness; windmills noisy/costly; capacity still small
08

Importance of Non-Conventional Sources

Why shift
  • Energy demand rising fast (industrialization, population) — conventional alone insufficient.
  • Conventional sources are non-renewable and bound to finish.
  • Conventional sources pollute and degrade the environment.
  • Large hydro disrupts wildlife, causes deforestation, serious resettlement problems.
  • Fossil fuels are chemical-industry feedstock — must be conserved for future generations.
