Solar Thermal Systems

How sunlight becomes heat: the conversion principle, the two collector families (flat-plate and concentrating), the three concentrator geometries, and a head-to-head comparison. This page pairs with the solar PV page that follows.

01

Two Ways to Use Solar Energy

Thermal vs PV

TSolar Thermal

Collect radiant heat; use it in a thermal system. Best for low-grade heat (comfort heating, hot water, preheating, drying, process heat up to ~180 °C).

PSolar Photovoltaic

Convert sunlight directly to electricity (Module 1, Page 3). No thermal step.

Conversion principle: solar radiation is shortwave light. When it strikes a solid/liquid it is absorbed and becomes heat — the material warms, stores heat, conducts it to surroundings (water/air), or reradiates to cooler materials. Same idea as the greenhouse effect.
02

Why Collectors?

Low density

Solar power density is low — about 1 kW/m² down to 0.1 kW/m². To gather useful heat you must cover a large ground area with collectors. The collector is the first unit in any solar thermal system: it absorbs solar energy as heat and transfers it efficiently to a heat-transport fluid, which delivers it to a storage tank / boiler / heat exchanger.

03

Flat-Plate Collector

Non-concentrating

Simple, stationary, no sun-tracking. Mechanically strong (rigid platform), withstands outdoor conditions (rain, storm), low maintenance. Disadvantage: no optical concentration → large heat-loss area, so high temperatures cannot be reached.

Five components

i. Absorber plate

Blackened plate (copper/aluminium/steel) that intercepts and absorbs incident radiation; coating minimises re-emission.

ii. Transparent cover

One or more glass/plastic sheets above the absorber. Lets radiation in but blocks reradiation + convective heat loss.

iii. Fluid tubes / channels

In thermal contact with the absorber; carry fluid that receives the heat.

iv. Thermal insulation

Under the absorber + tubes; cuts heat loss by transmission/convection.

v. Tight container / box

Encloses and protects all components.

Characteristic features

  • Absorbs direct and diffuse radiation
  • No tracking → stronger, simpler, low maintenance
Modified flat-plate: plain "booster" mirrors at the edges reflect extra radiation onto the absorber, raising concentration ratio from 1 to ~4.
Fig · Flat-Plate Collector — cross-section

Sunlight passes the glazing, is absorbed by the black plate, and heats fluid in the tubes; insulation limits loss. No tracking required.

04

Concentrating Collectors (Solar Concentrators)

Focus type

Converge radiation from a large area to a small area using mirrored/optical surfaces. Only beam radiation (unique direction, straight-line) can be concentrated; diffuse radiation has no direction and cannot be concentrated — a key disadvantage versus flat-plate.

  • Advantage: high temperatures (concentration → high-grade heat).
  • Disadvantage: need tracking (frequent maintenance); only work in direct sun (poor on hazy/cloudy days); need a receiver at focal point/line; heat-transfer fluid flows through receiver.

05

The Three Concentrator Geometries

Detail

TParabolic trough

"U"-shaped; concentrates sunlight onto a receiver tube along the focal line. Glass envelope may surround the tube to cut loss. Reflector: polished aluminium, silvered glass, or aluminized plastic. Needs sun on the plane through focal line + vertex.

DParabolic dish

Paraboloidal (parabola rotated about axis) → point focus. Two-axis tracking: rotate about vertical axis (alignment) + about horizontal axis (elevation). Concentration 10–1000×; temperatures to ~3000 °C.

CCentral tower

Receiver atop a tower; many independently 2-axis heliostats on the ground reflect sun onto it from all sides — acting as one huge dish. Thousands of heliostats; concentration up to ~3000×. Heat sent to a heat engine.

07

Solar Pond

Salinity-gradient collector

A solar pond is a large, shallow pool of water (typically 1–2 m deep) that collects and stores solar heat without expensive piping or pumped fluid. It works on a salinity gradient: the bottom layer is made very salty (high concentration), the top is fresh, with a stable gradient between. The salty bottom water is denser, so it does not convect upward and mix — it traps the absorbed heat at the bottom instead of letting it escape to the surface.

Sunlight penetrates the clear upper layers and is absorbed by the dark, salty bottom, which can reach 70–90 °C. The hot brine is drawn off to run a low-temperature turbine/ORC (organic Rankine cycle) generator, or used directly for process heating, desalination, or drying.

Why it matters: a solar pond is a cheap thermal store — it both collects and stores heat for hours/days, smoothing out the day–night cycle. The cost is mostly the pond liner and salt, not precision hardware. Limitations: land area, evaporation, and keeping the gradient stable.
