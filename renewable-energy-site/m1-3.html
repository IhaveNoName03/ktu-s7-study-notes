Solar Photovoltaic Systems

Sunlight straight to electricity. This page covers the photovoltaic effect, solar-cell construction, the I–V characteristic with fill factor and efficiency, the cell→module→panel→array hierarchy, shadowing/hot-spot, and stand-alone vs grid-connected systems.

01

What Is a Solar PV System?

Overview

Converts solar energy directly to electricity via the photovoltaic (PV) cell — a p-n junction current source driven by a flux of radiation. First made in 1954 for space satellites; terrestrial use grew after the 1973 oil crisis.

Advantages

  • No moving parts; direct conversion (no thermal-mechanical link)
  • Reliable, modular, durable, maintenance-free, quiet
  • Responds instantly to sunlight; ~20-year life
  • Located at point of use → minimal distribution network

Disadvantages

  • High cell cost → not yet competitive with conventional
  • Low efficiency + low radiation density → large area needed
  • Intermittent → needs storage for non-sun hours (adds cost)
02

The Photovoltaic Effect

Principle

Definition: when light strikes two dissimilar materials in close contact (a p-n junction of silicon/germanium), the photons provide the energy (e.m.f.) to free bound electrons, producing an electrical charge.

Sunlight = packets of energy called photons (different wavelengths). A photon absorbed in the depletion zone of a p-n junction dislodges an electron, creating a free electron–hole pair. The electron crosses the junction (assisted by the built-in field), travels through the external load, and the hole migrates to the back contact — restoring neutrality. Current flows as long as light falls on the junction.

p-n junction is kept with thin n-type on top (light passes through) over thicker p-type bulk. Generation of current happens in the depletion zone.
03

Solar Cell Construction

Layer stack

Parts

  • p-type silicon bulk (~100–350 µm)
  • thin n-type layer (~2 µm) diffused on top → p-n junction
  • front metallic grid (negative terminal)
  • opaque back metal contact (positive terminal)

Working

Photons free electrons at the junction; electrons flow n→external load→p, holes flow to back contact. The cell is essentially a large-area p-n junction.

Light creates electron–hole pairs at the junction; electrons exit the n-side through the grid, travel the external load, and return at the p-side back contact.

04

I–V Characteristic & Maximum Power

Core graph

Output power P = V × I. To get maximum power you operate at the point where the hyperbola P=VI is tangent to the I–V curve — the maximum-power point Pm (Vm, Im). Operating anywhere else wastes solar energy as heat.

Fill factor (FF)

Quality metric = how much of the rectangle (Voc × Isc) is actually used. Ideal FF = 1.

TermMeaning
VocOpen-circuit voltage (no load)
IscShort-circuit current (zero load)
Vm, ImVoltage, current at max power point
FFFF = (Vm × Im) / (Voc × Isc); typical 0.5–0.83

Improve FF by: (i) increasing photocurrent & decreasing reverse-saturation current; (ii) minimising internal series resistance; (iii) maximising shunt resistance.

Effect of insolation & temperature

  • Insolation ↑ → Isc rises (curve shifts up); Voc rises slightly → more power.
  • Temperature ↑ → Voc falls (diode leakage) → power & FF drop. So cells perform better when cool.
Efficiency η: η = (max output Vm·Im) / (solar energy incident on cell). Low in practice → large area needed.
05

Cell → Module → Panel → Array

Hierarchy

Solar cell

Single p-n junction; tiny output; no protection. Needs encapsulation.

Solar module

32–36 cells in series (to charge a 12 V battery). Cells hermetically sealed; encapsulated in EVA (insulator, transparent, low water absorption), glass front, PVF/tedlar rear. Cells from same batch to minimise mismatch loss.

Solar panel

Modules wired in series (same current) and parallel (same voltage). Frame mounts them. Bypass diodes across each module (skip a faulty one); blocking diodes in series strings (stop back-feed into a failed string).

Solar array

Panels installed in a field, stationary or tracked, spaced so no panel shadows its neighbour and maintenance is easy.

06

Effect of Shadowing (Hot-Spot)

Failure mode

Partial shadowing can completely damage a module via the hot-spot effect. In a series string:

  • A partially shadowed cell stops producing; the lit cells forward-bias it, driving a large circulating current through the small shadowed area → excessive heating.
  • A fully shadowed cell in a short-circuited string: the n lit cells' voltage appears as reverse bias (nV) across it. If peak inverse voltage (PIV) is exceeded, current flows and dissipates large power → damage. Risk grows with string length.

Fix: connect a bypass diode across the affected cell for an alternate current path. International practice: one bypass diode per ~18 crystalline-silicon cells → a 34–36-cell module has two bypass diodes in its terminal box. (During healthy operation the diode is reverse-biased and idle, with a small leakage loss.)

08

Cell Generations

Technology

1First gen

Wafer-based mono- & multi-crystalline silicon. Dominates the market today.

2Second gen

Thin-film: amorphous silicon, CIGS, cadmium telluride. Commercially available.

3Third gen

Different principle: dye-sensitized, organic, quantum-dot. Under development.
