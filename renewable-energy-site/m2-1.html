Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC)

Power from the temperature difference between warm surface seawater and cold deep seawater. This page covers the principle (with the absorption law + Carnot limit), the three plant cycles, site selection, biofouling, and the pros/cons.

OTEC Open/Closed/Hybrid Carnot Biofouling
01

Principle of OTEC

Heat engine at sea

Ocean surface water absorbs solar radiation and warms; deep water stays cold. This creates a temperature gradient that a heat engine can exploit: surface water = heat source (~27 °C), deep water = heat sink (~4–7 °C). A minimum ΔT of 20 °C is required for the engine to work effectively.

Absorption law (Lampert's law)

Each equal-thickness layer of water absorbs the same fraction of solar heat as radiation passes through:

dI/dy = −µ·I₀  →  integrating:   I = I₀·e^(−µy)

where I₀ = intensity at y=0, µ = absorption coefficient, y = depth. No convective mixing occurs between the warm light top layer and cold heavy bottom layer, so the two act as infinite reservoirs at ~27 °C (source) and ~4–7 °C (sink).

Carnot efficiency limit

η = (T₁ − T₂) / T₁  (T in Kelvin)

Example: T₁ = 27 °C = 300 K, T₂ = 7 °C = 280 K → η = (300−280)/300 = 6.67%. Low, because the gradient is small — characteristic of OTEC.

02

The Three Plant Cycles

Open · Closed · Hybrid

OOpen cycle
(Claude)

Warm surface water is flash-evaporated at low pressure (water boils at lower T under low pressure). The steam expands through a turbine → mechanical energy, then is condensed by cold deep seawater. Working fluid = seawater itself.

CClosed cycle
(Anderson)

Warm surface water evaporates a low-boiling refrigerant (ammonia/Freon) in a heat exchanger → vapor drives a turbine → generator. Cold seawater condenses the vapor; refrigerant is recycled. Working fluid stays separate from seawater.

HHybrid

Combines both: warm seawater is flash-evaporated in a vacuum (open step); that vapor then evaporates a working fluid (ammonia) in a separate closed loop → turbine → electricity. The vaporized seawater is condensed into freshwater by cold seawater (bonus: desalination).

Closed-cycle is the most common because it avoids the huge turbine needed for low-density steam in the open cycle; hybrid adds freshwater production.
Fig · Closed-cycle (Anderson) OTEC

Warm surface water boils ammonia in the evaporator → vapor drives the turbine/generator → cold deep water condenses the ammonia → pumped back. Working fluid never mixes with seawater.

03

Site Selection for OTEC

Where it works

Multi-criteria decision analysis (e.g. TOPSIS) picks optimal sites using technical, environmental, and socioeconomic criteria:

  • Thermal potential: evaluate 16-year sea-surface-temperature data; compute % of time the power threshold (ΔT ≥ 20 °C) is met.
  • Feasibility: decision matrix over technical / environmental / socioeconomic factors.
  • Site identification: typically tropical/subtropical deep-ocean sites close to land with a steep continental shelf (so cold deep water is near the surface plant).
  • Cost: compare OTEC levelized cost of energy (LCOE) against other technologies.
Need: warm surface water + access to cold deep water nearby + grid/load proximity. Tropical islands are ideal.
04

Biofouling

Efficiency killer

Definition: deposition and growth of microorganisms inside the evaporator and condenser heat-exchanger pipes. It (i) reduces heat-transfer area efficiency and (ii) lowers performance.

More heat-transfer surface = more area for organisms to attach, so it scales with plant size.

Control

  • Mechanical: continuous circulation of close-fitting cleaning balls.
  • Chemical: chlorination / biocide additives. Release can be held to ~1/10 of the environmental limit, but biocides still risk pollution.
05

Advantages & Limitations of OTEC

Balanced

Advantages

  • Renewable, baseload-capable (ocean temp steady day/night)
  • Non-polluting; large resource in tropics
  • Hybrid variant produces freshwater (desalination co-benefit)
  • Uses existing heat-engine technology

Limitations

  • Very low Carnot efficiency (~3–7%) → huge plant for little power
  • Needs ΔT ≥ 20 °C + deep cold water nearby → few sites
  • Biofouling cuts heat-exchanger performance
  • Long pipes to deep water; marine corrosion; high capital cost
  • Cold-water pumping energy eats into net output
