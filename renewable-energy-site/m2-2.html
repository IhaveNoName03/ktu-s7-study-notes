Tidal Energy

Power from the rise and fall of the sea. This page covers the origin of tides (Moon vs Sun), spring/neap, the components of a tidal plant, the four operating modes, single vs double basin, site criteria, and the environmental impacts/limitations.

Tides Spring/Neap Basin types Site criteria
01

Origin of Tides

Gravity

Tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun on the rotating Earth. The Moon is nearer, so its pull is ~2.33× stronger than the Sun's.

  • Water facing the Moon is pulled toward it → high tide on that side.
  • A corresponding high tide forms on the opposite side (Earth pulled away from the water).
  • The Moon takes 24 h 50 min per rotation → two high tides ~12 h 25 min apart.
  • Sun's effect is the same but ~2.2× weaker and Earth orbits it in 24 h, so solar and lunar tides drift in/out of phase.

02

Tidal Energy & Potential

The resource

The energy source is the difference in water level between high and low tide (the tidal range, typically 5–15 m). In a plant, a reservoir behind a barrage fills at high tide; stored water flows out through turbines to the sea at low tide, driving generators.

Potential energy of a basin: for entrapped water of surface area A and tidal range R, a thin layer of height dh at head h has potential energy dE = ρ·g·A·h·dh (ρ = water density, g = gravity). Integrating over the full range gives the storable energy; minimum workable range ≈ 5 m.

03

Components of a Tidal Power Plant

Hardware

i. Dam / dyke

Barrier between sea and basin (and between basins if >1). Holds the head.

ii. Sluiceways

Gated channels in the dam; let water in at high tide and drain the basin at low tide.

iii. Powerhouse

Holds turbines + generators + auxiliaries. High-head water runs the turbines → generators produce power.

Classification: Basin Systems

Single vs double

Single basin

One basin behind a dam. Turbines in the dam. Sub-types:

  • Single ebb: fills via sluice at high tide, generates on ebb (intermittent).
  • Single tide: water enters through turbines at high tide; stops as sea falls.
  • Double cycle: reversible turbines → generate on both filling and emptying.

Double basin

Two basins at different levels, dams between basins and sea. Upper basin kept higher (filled at high tide); lower basin discharges at low tide. Turbines sit between the basins; water flows upper→lower, generating continuously (smoother output than single basin).

Fig · Double-basin Tidal Power Plant

Sea fills the upper basin at high tide; water flows upper→lower basin through the turbine (generating); the lower basin discharges to the sea at low tide. Two basins give steadier output than one.

06

Site Requirements & Feasibility

Where to build

Site requirements

  • High tidal range (≥ 5 m economic)
  • Short dam length for a large basin → narrow estuary/bay inlet
  • Near the load centre
  • Protected from high waves
  • Must not block shipping

Feasibility factors

  • Tidal range ≥ 5 m
  • Low dam-construction cost for needed storage
  • No interruption to shipping
  • Protected from storm waves
  • Low silt content (avoid sedimentation cost)
India sites: Gulf of Kutch, Gulf of Khambhat, Gulf of Cambay (Gujarat), Sundarbans delta & Pitts Creek (West Bengal).
07

Merits & Limitations

Balanced

Merits

  • Pollution-free, inexhaustible
  • Dam at bays — no village uprooting / ecology disruption like large hydro
  • No land submergence; basins usable for fish farming
  • Not weather-dependent (tide cycle is definite, unlike monsoon)

Limitations

  • Needs natural sites; good ones are far from load → high transmission cost
  • Uneven output (variable head); not continuous (timing of tides)
  • Long construction; costly basin/plant
  • Siltation; corrosion from seawater shortens equipment life
  • Low head → many turbines needed; efficiency varies with range
  • Power varies with lunar cycle
